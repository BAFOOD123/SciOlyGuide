## How the event runs

Most tests are a story: "Forty people got sick after a church picnic…" followed by a line list, an epi curve and a food-history table. You'll classify the outbreak, write a case definition, calculate attack rates and relative risk, choose a study design, identify biases and recommend control measures. Other sections test vocabulary, the history of epidemiology, surveillance and prevention levels.

Free-response answers earn points for **specific, well-reasoned** explanations. "Wash hands" scores less than "exclude the infected food handler until two negative stool samples, and retrain staff on hand hygiene."

## Background and surveillance

### Clinical vs public health approach

| | Clinical medicine | Public health (epidemiology) |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | The **individual** patient | The **population** |
| Diagnosis | Of one patient | Of a community's health (rates, patterns) |
| Treatment | Drugs, surgery for that person | Interventions: vaccines, sanitation, policy, education |
| Question | "What's wrong with this person?" | "Who is getting sick, where, when, and why?" |

**Epidemiology** is the study of the **distribution** (frequency and pattern) and **determinants** (causes and risk factors) of health-related states or events in specified populations, and the **application** of this study to control health problems (the CDC definition).

### History of epidemiology

| Who | When | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| **Hippocrates** | ~400 BCE | Linked disease to environment and lifestyle (*Airs, Waters, Places*); "endemic" and "epidemic" |
| **John Graunt** | 1662 | Analyzed London's Bills of Mortality: first life tables and patterns of death |
| **James Lind** | 1747 | Early controlled trial: citrus cured scurvy in sailors |
| **Edward Jenner** | 1796 | Smallpox vaccine from cowpox |
| **Ignaz Semmelweis** | 1847 | Handwashing cut deaths from childbed fever |
| **John Snow** | **1854** | **"Father of epidemiology."** Mapped cholera deaths around the **Broad Street pump** in London, had the handle removed, and compared water companies |
| **William Farr** | 1800s | Vital statistics and surveillance in England |
| **Florence Nightingale** | 1850s | Statistical graphics (polar area charts) on soldier deaths |
| **Robert Koch** | 1880s | **Koch's postulates**; identified the agents of TB and cholera |
| **Louis Pasteur** | 1860s–80s | Germ theory, rabies and anthrax vaccines |
| **Joseph Goldberger** | 1910s | Showed pellagra was dietary (niacin), not infectious |
| **Doll and Hill** | 1950 | Case-control and cohort studies linking smoking to lung cancer |
| **Framingham Heart Study** | 1948– | Long-running cohort that identified cardiovascular risk factors |
| **Smallpox eradication** | 1980 | WHO declared smallpox eradicated (last natural case 1977) using surveillance and **ring vaccination** |

### Core epidemiologic functions

Public health surveillance, field investigation, analytic studies, evaluation, linkages and policy development. **The public health approach**: (1) define the problem (surveillance), (2) identify risk factors, (3) develop and test interventions, (4) implement them and monitor effectiveness.

### Natural history and spectrum of disease

![Natural history of disease timeline](img:dd-natural-history)

- **Stages:** susceptibility → **exposure** → subclinical disease (pathologic changes, no symptoms) → **onset of symptoms** → clinical disease → recovery, disability or death.
- **Incubation period** (infectious diseases) or **latency period** (chronic diseases): time from exposure to symptoms.
- **Spectrum:** many infections are **inapparent (subclinical)**. The **iceberg concept**: reported cases are the tip; most infections are mild or asymptomatic. **Carriers** can spread disease without symptoms (incubatory, convalescent, chronic: "Typhoid Mary" Mallon).
- **Causes of disease:** infectious agents (bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists, parasites, **prions**: misfolded proteins like those of mad cow disease and CJD) and noninfectious causes (chemicals, toxins, injuries, radiation, nutritional, genetic, behavioral).

### Key terms (from the CDC glossary)

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Endemic** | The usual, constant level of a disease in an area |
| **Hyperendemic** | Persistently high levels |
| **Sporadic** | Occasional, irregular cases |
| **Epidemic** | More cases than expected in an area over a period of time |
| **Outbreak** | An epidemic in a more limited area (often used for a local epidemic) |
| **Cluster** | Cases grouped in place and time, which may or may not exceed expected numbers |
| **Pandemic** | An epidemic spreading over several countries or continents |
| **Reservoir** | Where an agent normally lives and multiplies (humans, animals, environment) |
| **Vector** | A living carrier, usually an arthropod (mosquito, tick), that transmits an agent |
| **Vehicle** | A nonliving carrier (food, water, blood, fomites) |
| **Fomite** | An object that carries an agent (doorknob, towel) |
| **Zoonosis** | An infection transmissible from animals to humans |
| **Index case** | The first case to come to investigators' attention; the **primary case** is the one that brought the infection into the population |
| **Secondary attack rate** | Cases among contacts of primary cases / total contacts |
| **Risk** | Probability that an event will occur in a given time |
| **Surveillance** | Ongoing, systematic collection, analysis and interpretation of health data |
| **Immunity, herd immunity** | Resistance to infection in individuals; indirect protection of a population when enough are immune |

### The epidemiologic triad and chain of transmission

![The epidemiologic triad](img:dd-triad)

**Triad:** **Agent** (the cause), **host** (the person: age, immunity, genetics, behavior) and **environment** (physical, biological, social factors that bring them together). For many diseases, **time** sits in the middle. Vectors can be added between the triad's corners.

**Chain of infection:** **agent → reservoir → portal of exit → mode of transmission → portal of entry → susceptible host.** Control measures break the chain at one or more links.

- **Direct transmission:** contact (touching, kissing, sexual), droplet spread (coughs; within ~1–2 m).
- **Indirect transmission:** **airborne** (droplet nuclei or dust that stay suspended), **vehicle-borne** (food, water, fomites), **vector-borne** (mechanical, or biological where the agent develops in the vector).

### Surveillance

**Steps of surveillance (CDC's 5 steps):**

1. Identify the health problem and set objectives.
2. **Collect** data.
3. **Analyze** (and validate) data.
4. **Interpret** results.
5. **Disseminate** findings, and **use** them for action (and evaluate the system).

**Types:**

| Type | How it works | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Passive** | Health providers send reports as required | Notifiable disease reporting |
| **Active** | Health department actively seeks cases | Calling hospitals during an outbreak |
| **Sentinel** | A selected group of sites reports | Influenza-like illness network (ILINet) |
| **Syndromic** | Tracks symptoms or behaviors before diagnosis | ER chief complaints, over-the-counter drug sales |
| Others | Lab-based, event-based, wastewater surveillance | PulseNet, COVID wastewater monitoring |

**Attributes of a good system:** simplicity, flexibility, data quality, acceptability, **sensitivity** (finds true cases), **predictive value positive**, representativeness, **timeliness**, stability.

## Outbreak investigation

### The steps

The CDC lists these steps (the order can change in a real investigation):

1. Prepare for field work.
2. **Establish the existence of an outbreak** (compare with expected numbers).
3. **Verify the diagnosis.**
4. **Construct a working case definition.**
5. **Find cases systematically** and record information (**line listing**).
6. Perform **descriptive epidemiology** (person, place, time).
7. **Develop hypotheses.**
8. **Evaluate hypotheses** epidemiologically (analytic studies).
9. As necessary, reconsider, refine and re-evaluate hypotheses.
10. Compare and reconcile with laboratory and environmental studies.
11. **Implement control and prevention measures** (as early as possible!).
12. Initiate or maintain surveillance.
13. **Communicate findings.**

### Case definitions

A case definition is a standard set of criteria for deciding whether a person has the condition, using:

- **Clinical criteria** (symptoms, lab results).
- **Person** (who), **place** (where) and **time** (when): restrictions on the population.

Cases are often classified as **confirmed** (lab-verified), **probable** (typical clinical features, no lab confirmation) and **possible/suspected**. A loose definition early on finds more cases (**sensitive**); a strict one later is more **specific**.

> [!TIP]
> Example: "A case is a person who attended the Smith wedding on June 3 and developed diarrhea (3+ loose stools in 24 hours) or vomiting between June 3 and June 7."

### Descriptive epidemiology: person, place, time

**Line listing:** one row per case, columns for ID, age, sex, onset date and time, symptoms, exposures, lab results.

**Epi curves** are histograms of cases by onset time:

![Epidemic curve showing a point-source outbreak](img:dd-epi-curve)

| Pattern | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Point source** | A sharp rise and fall, all cases within about one incubation period | A single contaminated meal |
| **Continuous common source** | Rises, then **plateaus** while the exposure continues; drops when it's removed | Contaminated water supply |
| **Intermittent common source** | Irregular peaks | A recurring exposure |
| **Propagated (progressive)** | A series of **successively larger peaks**, about one incubation period apart (person-to-person spread) | Measles in a school |
| **Mixed** | A point source followed by secondary person-to-person cases | Norovirus at a buffet, then household spread |

**Using an epi curve with a known incubation period:** for a point-source outbreak, count back from the **peak** by the **average** (or median) incubation period, or from the first case by the **minimum** and from the last case by the **maximum**, to estimate the **exposure time**.

**Place:** spot maps (John Snow's), area maps with rates (choropleth), and **cluster maps**.

### Molecular epidemiology

- **PFGE (pulsed-field gel electrophoresis):** bacterial DNA cut with rare-cutting restriction enzymes, separated in a gel with alternating electric fields. Isolates with **matching band patterns** are likely from the same source.
- **Whole genome sequencing (WGS)** and **SNP analysis:** compare genomes base by base; isolates differing by only a few SNPs are closely related. WGS has replaced PFGE as more precise.
- **PulseNet:** the CDC's national network of public health and food regulatory labs that compares the "DNA fingerprints" of foodborne bacteria (*Salmonella*, *E. coli* O157, *Listeria*) to find multistate outbreaks that wouldn't be noticed locally.

### Study designs

![Hierarchy of epidemiologic study designs](img:dd-studies)

| Design | How | Measure | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Randomized controlled trial (experimental)** | Investigator **assigns** exposure randomly | Relative risk, efficacy | Gold standard; controls confounding | Expensive, ethical limits, may not generalize |
| **Cohort (prospective or retrospective)** | Start from **exposure**, follow to see who gets the disease | **Relative risk**, attack rate | Can study multiple outcomes; shows time order; calculates incidence | Expensive and slow for rare diseases; loss to follow-up |
| **Case-control** | Start from **disease** (cases and controls), look back at exposure | **Odds ratio** | Fast, cheap, good for **rare diseases** and many exposures | **Recall bias**, choosing proper controls, can't calculate incidence |
| **Cross-sectional** | Measure exposure and disease **at one time** (a survey) | Prevalence, prevalence ratio | Quick snapshot | Can't show which came first |
| **Ecological** | Compare **groups**, not individuals | Correlation | Uses existing data, cheap | **Ecological fallacy** (group patterns may not hold for individuals) |
| Case report / series | Describe one or a few patients | — | Spots new diseases | No comparison group |

**Outbreak tip:** in a **small, well-defined group** (everyone at a wedding), use a **retrospective cohort** and calculate attack rates for everyone. In a **large or poorly defined population** (a community *Salmonella* outbreak), use a **case-control** study.

### Measures of association

**The 2 × 2 table:**

| | Ill | Not ill | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exposed** | a | b | a + b |
| **Unexposed** | c | d | c + d |

- **Attack rate** (exposed) = a / (a + b); (unexposed) = c / (c + d).
- **Relative risk (RR)** = [a / (a + b)] / [c / (c + d)] (cohort studies).
- **Odds ratio (OR)** = **ad / bc** (case-control studies). It approximates RR when the disease is rare.
- **Attributable risk** (risk difference) = AR_exposed − AR_unexposed. **Attributable risk percent** = (RR − 1)/RR × 100%.
- **Interpreting:** RR or OR = 1 means no association; **> 1** means exposure is a risk factor; **< 1** means it's protective.
- **Confidence intervals (95% CI):** if the interval for RR or OR **includes 1**, the association **isn't statistically significant**. A narrow interval means a precise estimate. (You need to interpret, not calculate, CIs.)

> [!TIP]
> Example: potato salad eaters: 40 ill of 50 (AR 80%). Non-eaters: 5 ill of 50 (AR 10%). RR = 0.80 / 0.10 = 8.0: eaters were 8 times as likely to get sick. The food with the **highest attack rate among eaters, lowest among non-eaters, highest RR, and that explains most cases** is the likely culprit.

### Causation

**Bradford Hill criteria (1965)** for judging whether an association is causal:

1. **Strength** of association (large RR).
2. **Consistency** across studies.
3. **Specificity** (one cause → one effect; the weakest criterion).
4. **Temporality** (cause before effect, the only **required** one).
5. **Biological gradient** (dose–response).
6. **Plausibility** (makes biological sense).
7. **Coherence** with known facts.
8. **Experiment** (removing the exposure reduces disease).
9. **Analogy** (similar causes have similar effects).

**Comparing causal models:**

| Model | Idea | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Koch's postulates** (1890) | (1) The microbe is found in all cases; (2) isolated and grown in pure culture; (3) causes the disease when introduced into a healthy host; (4) re-isolated from that host | Fails for asymptomatic carriers, microbes that can't be cultured (viruses, some bacteria), multifactorial and noninfectious diseases |
| **Evans's postulates** (1976) | Updated, epidemiological: higher prevalence and incidence in the exposed; exposure precedes disease; a spectrum of responses; host immune response; prevention reduces disease | Still mainly for infectious disease |
| **Bradford Hill** | Weigh evidence for any exposure | Not a checklist; judgement needed |
| **Sufficient-component cause model** (Rothman's "causal pies") | Disease occurs when a full "pie" of component causes is present; a **necessary cause** is in every pie | Conceptual, hard to measure |
| **Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs)** | Arrows map assumed causal relationships, showing which variables are **confounders** to adjust for and which (colliders, mediators) not to | Depends on assumptions being right |
| **GRADE** | Rates the **certainty of a body of evidence** (high, moderate, low, very low); RCTs start high, observational studies start low, and ratings move up or down (bias, inconsistency, large effects, dose-response) | A framework for evidence, not a single study |

### Bias, confounding and error

- **Random error:** chance; reduced with larger samples (and reflected in CIs and p-values).
- **Selection bias:** study groups differ systematically (healthy worker effect, volunteers, loss to follow-up, choosing hospital controls).
- **Information bias:** errors in measuring exposure or outcome: **recall bias** (cases remember exposures better than controls), **interviewer bias**, **misclassification** (differential or non-differential), observation bias.
- **Confounding:** a third variable is associated with **both** the exposure and the disease, and isn't on the causal path. (Example: coffee appears linked to lung cancer because coffee drinkers smoke more.) Controlled by **randomization, restriction, matching** (design) or **stratification, adjustment** (analysis).
- **Stratification:** split the data by the confounder (smokers vs non-smokers). If the stratum-specific RRs are similar to each other but different from the crude RR, there's confounding. If the strata RRs differ from each other, there's **effect modification** (interaction).
- **Adjusted (standardized) rates:** **direct standardization** applies each population's age-specific rates to a standard population, so populations with different age structures can be compared fairly.

**Control and comparison groups:** controls should come from the **same source population** as cases and be free of the disease. Matching on age or sex helps.

### Vaccine effectiveness

- **Vaccine efficacy** (measured in a **randomized trial**, ideal conditions) and **vaccine effectiveness** (in **real-world** observational studies) use the same formula:

**VE = (ARU − ARV) / ARU × 100% = (1 − RR) × 100%**

where ARU = attack rate in the unvaccinated and ARV = attack rate in the vaccinated.

- **Case-control:** VE ≈ (1 − OR) × 100%. A common design is the **test-negative design** (people tested for the disease; cases test positive, controls test negative).
- **Screening method:** compares the proportion of cases vaccinated with the proportion of the population vaccinated.

> [!TIP]
> Example: unvaccinated attack rate 20%, vaccinated 2%. VE = (20 − 2) / 20 = 90%.

### Herd immunity and reproduction numbers

- **Basic reproduction number R₀:** average number of secondary cases from one case in a **fully susceptible** population (measles ~12–18, polio ~5–7, seasonal flu ~1.3).
- **Effective reproduction number Rₜ (or R):** in the actual population, with immunity and interventions. **R = R₀ × (fraction susceptible)**. If R > 1, the outbreak grows; **R < 1**, it shrinks.
- **Herd immunity threshold:** **HIT = 1 − 1/R₀**. Measles with R₀ = 15: 1 − 1/15 ≈ 93% must be immune.
- **Vaccination coverage needed:** V_c = HIT / vaccine effectiveness.

> [!TIP]
> Example: R₀ = 4 gives HIT = 75%. With a 90% effective vaccine, you need 0.75 / 0.90 ≈ 83% coverage.

### Control measures (National emphasis)

- **Control the source:** remove contaminated food, close a pool, treat or isolate cases.
- **Interrupt transmission:** sanitation, vector control, handwashing, masks.
- **Protect susceptible hosts:** vaccination, prophylaxis (post-exposure antibiotics), education.
- **Isolation** (separates the **sick**) vs **quarantine** (restricts the movement of the **exposed but well**).

## Patterns, control and prevention

### Measures of frequency

| Measure | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Ratio** | x / y | Numerator not part of denominator (males per 100 females) |
| **Proportion** | x / (x + y) | Numerator is part of the denominator |
| **Rate** | Events / population **over time**, × 10ⁿ | Time is built in |
| **Incidence proportion (attack rate, risk)** | New cases during a period / population at risk at the start | Outbreaks |
| **Incidence rate (incidence density)** | New cases / **person-time** at risk | When follow-up differs |
| **Secondary attack rate** | Cases among contacts / total contacts | Household spread |
| **Point prevalence** | All existing cases at a point in time / population | **Prevalence ≈ incidence × duration** |
| **Period prevalence** | Existing + new cases over a period / average population | |
| **Crude death (mortality) rate** | Deaths / population × 1,000 or 100,000 (per year) | |
| **Cause-specific mortality rate** | Deaths from a cause / population | |
| **Case-fatality rate (proportion)** | Deaths from a disease / cases of that disease × 100% | Severity |
| **Proportionate mortality** | Deaths from a cause / all deaths | |
| **Infant mortality rate** | Infant deaths < 1 year / live births × 1,000 | |
| **Years of potential life lost** | Sum of (reference age − age at death) | Weights early deaths |

### Reading charts and tables

Be ready for: line graphs of trends (look for seasonality, secular trends, sudden changes), bar charts by age or sex, maps, survival curves, box plots, and dual-axis graphs. Read axes and units first. Also know **arithmetic vs logarithmic** scales (on a log scale, a straight line means a constant rate of change).

### Levels of prevention

![Levels of prevention](img:dd-prevention)

| Level | Goal | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primordial** | Prevent risk factors from developing in the first place | Policies for walkable towns, healthy school food, tobacco taxes |
| **Primary** | Prevent disease before it starts (in the susceptible) | **Vaccination**, seatbelts, condoms, clean water, helmets |
| **Secondary** | **Early detection** and treatment (before symptoms or early in disease) | **Screening**: mammograms, Pap tests, blood pressure checks, newborn screening |
| **Tertiary** | Reduce complications and disability in established disease | Rehabilitation, cardiac rehab, diabetic foot care |
| Quaternary | Avoid harm from unnecessary medical care | Avoiding over-testing and overtreatment |

**Strategies of disease control:** **containment** (limit spread), **elimination** (zero cases in a region: measles in the U.S. in 2000), **eradication** (zero cases worldwide permanently: smallpox; nearly polio and Guinea worm).

**Scope of prevention:** individual (behavior change), community (programs, environmental changes), and policy or regulation (laws, taxes, standards). The **health impact pyramid** (Frieden) puts socioeconomic factors at the base (largest impact) and counseling at the top (smallest).

### Evaluating interventions (National)

Compare **pre- and post-intervention** data. Ask: did the rate fall more than the trend or comparison area did? Was the change significant? Could something else explain it (secular trends, regression to the mean, a change in case definition or reporting)? Strengths and weaknesses of a strategy include cost, reach, acceptability, equity, sustainability and side effects.

## How to study

1. **Read the CDC's free *Principles of Epidemiology in Public Health Practice* (3rd ed.), Lessons 1–6.** It's the source of the glossary and much of the test.
2. **Do the formulas until automatic:** attack rate, RR, OR, VE, HIT, prevalence, mortality rates.
3. **Work CDC case studies** (the CDC's free "Epidemiology case studies" and outbreak training exercises such as "Oswego"). They're nearly identical to event scenarios.
4. **Practice explaining:** write full answers to "what bias might affect this study?" and "what control measures would you recommend?"

**The notes sheet:** steps of an outbreak investigation, the 2 × 2 table and every formula, study-design comparison table, bias types, Bradford Hill, Koch vs Evans, epi-curve shapes, levels of prevention, surveillance types and attributes, and a history timeline.

## Common pitfalls

- Calculating an RR for a case-control study (use the OR).
- Mixing up incidence and prevalence.
- Forgetting the **time element** in a rate.
- Confusing **isolation** (sick) and **quarantine** (exposed).
- Saying a CI that includes 1 shows an effect.
- Recommending only vague controls. Be specific: what, who, how.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>1. 100 cases and 200 controls. 60 cases and 40 controls ate a suspect food. What's the OR?</summary>

a = 60, b = 40 (exposed controls), c = 40 (unexposed cases), d = 160. OR = (60 × 160) / (40 × 40) = 6.0.

</details>

<details><summary>2. A disease has R₀ = 5. What's the herd immunity threshold?</summary>

1 − 1/5 = 80%.

</details>

<details><summary>3. An epi curve shows successive peaks about a week apart, each larger. What pattern is it?</summary>

Propagated (person-to-person) spread.

</details>

<details><summary>4. Prevalence of a disease rises but incidence stays the same. Suggest a reason.</summary>

Duration increased, for example from a new treatment that keeps people alive longer without curing them.

</details>

<details><summary>5. Which Bradford Hill criterion is required?</summary>

Temporality: the cause must come before the effect.

</details>

<details><summary>6. Mammograms are what level of prevention?</summary>

Secondary (screening for early detection).

</details>
