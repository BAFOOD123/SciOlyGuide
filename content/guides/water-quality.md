## How the event runs

A written test covers ecology, coral reef organisms (ID by common name from photos), and interpreting water-monitoring data. **No wet lab tests** are done by you except the salinometer. Build a dense **notes sheet** and a **salinometer** you've calibrated. Split the work: one partner masters organism ID, the other masters the chemistry and monitoring data.

## Part I: Marine and estuary ecology (30%)

### Core concepts

- **The water cycle:** evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, runoff, infiltration (see also Dynamic Planet). **Watersheds** drain to a common water body; what happens on land reaches the sea.
- **Estuaries:** where **freshwater rivers meet the salty ocean** (brackish water). Types: salt marshes, mangroves, deltas, lagoons, fjords. They are among the **most productive** ecosystems, serving as **nurseries** for fish and shellfish, filtering pollutants, buffering storms, and supporting migratory birds. Examples: Chesapeake Bay, San Francisco Bay.
- **Salinity zones:** freshwater (< 0.5 ppt), oligohaline, mesohaline, polyhaline, marine (~35 ppt). A **salt wedge** forms where denser seawater slides under fresh river water. Organisms are **stenohaline** (narrow salinity tolerance) or **euryhaline** (wide).

### Aquatic chemistry and life

- **Dissolved oxygen (DO):** vital for aquatic life. Higher in **cold, turbulent, less salty** water. Lowered by decay, warmth and high salinity.
- **pH:** seawater is slightly basic (~8.1). The **carbonate buffer system** (CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ HCO₃⁻ ⇌ CO₃²⁻) resists pH change and supplies carbonate for shells and coral.
- **Nutrients:** nitrogen and phosphorus are limiting nutrients; too much causes **eutrophication**.
- **Nutrient cycling:** the **nitrogen cycle** (fixation, nitrification, assimilation, ammonification, denitrification) and **phosphorus cycle** (from rock weathering, no gas phase).

### Food webs and interactions

- **Producers:** phytoplankton (diatoms, dinoflagellates), algae, seagrasses, mangroves. **Primary consumers:** zooplankton, grazers. Then larger consumers up to apex predators. **Decomposers** recycle nutrients.
- **Energy flow:** ~10% transfers between trophic levels. **Microbial loop** recycles dissolved organic matter.
- **Community interactions:** competition, predation, **symbiosis** (mutualism like coral–zooxanthellae and cleaner shrimp; commensalism; parasitism), keystone species.
- **Population dynamics:** exponential vs logistic growth, carrying capacity (K), limiting factors.

### Water treatment

- **Potable water treatment:** coagulation/flocculation → sedimentation → filtration → **disinfection** (chlorine, UV, ozone).
- **Wastewater treatment:** **primary** (screening and settling solids) → **secondary** (microbes consume organic matter, activated sludge) → **tertiary** (nutrient removal, disinfection).

### Applied topics

- **Pollution:** **sedimentation** (smothers reefs and clogs gills), nutrient runoff, **harmful algal blooms (HABs)** (red tides from dinoflagellates; cyanobacteria; produce toxins and dead zones), **emerging contaminants** (pharmaceuticals, **PFAS "forever chemicals"**, microplastics), oil, thermal pollution.
- **The five ocean garbage patches:** rotating gyres accumulate plastic — North and South Pacific, North and South Atlantic, and Indian Ocean. The **Great Pacific Garbage Patch** is the largest.
- **Harmful species / invasives:** lionfish (Atlantic/Caribbean), green crabs, zebra mussels; **crown-of-thorns starfish** outbreaks on reefs.
- **Coral decline:** bleaching, disease, recently killed coral.
- **Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK):** knowledge held by Indigenous and local communities built over generations of observation. Apply it to management scenarios (e.g., traditional fishing closures, seasonal rounds, and stewardship practices that complement scientific data).

### State and National: life history

- **Age structure** and **survivorship curves:** Type I (low early mortality, like mammals), Type II (constant, like some birds), **Type III** (high early mortality, like most fish and invertebrates that produce many offspring).
- **Life tables** track survival and reproduction by age.
- **r-strategists** (many small offspring, little care, fast: most fish, invertebrates) vs **K-strategists** (few offspring, much care, long-lived: large predators). **Succession** in aquatic communities.

## Part II: Coral reef identification (30%)

![A coral reef ecosystem](img:wq-coral-reef)

### Coral biology

- **Hard/stony corals (Scleractinia)** build reefs by secreting **calcium carbonate (aragonite)** skeletons. The animal is a **polyp** (a cnidarian) living in symbiosis with **zooxanthellae** (photosynthetic dinoflagellates) that give corals food and color.
- **Reef types:** fringing (along shore), barrier (separated by a lagoon; the Great Barrier Reef), and **atolls** (rings around a sunken island — Darwin's subsidence theory).
- **Bleaching:** heat stress (or pollution) makes corals expel their zooxanthellae, turning white; prolonged bleaching kills them. **Ocean acidification** (more CO₂ → lower pH → less carbonate) weakens skeletons.
- **Reef health indicators:** herbivores that control algae (parrotfish, urchins) are good signs; crown-of-thorns outbreaks, fleshy algae overgrowth and dead coral are bad signs.

### Organisms to know (common name ID, plus ecology, life cycle, feeding)

**Worldwide:**

| Organism | Role / notes |
|---|---|
| **Banded coral shrimp** (*Stenopus hispidus*) | Cleaner shrimp; removes parasites from fish (mutualism) |
| **Butterflyfish** (*Chaetodon*) | Coral polyp feeders; their presence indicates healthy coral |
| **Crown-of-thorns starfish** (*Acanthaster planci*) | Coral predator; **outbreaks devastate reefs** |
| **Fleshy algae** (red, green, brown) | Overgrows and competes with coral when herbivores decline |
| **Grouper >30 cm** (Serranidae) | Large predator; overfished; indicates a healthy reef |
| **Hard/stony corals** (Scleractinia) | The reef builders |
| **Lobsters** (Nephropidae) | Scavengers/predators; overfished |
| **Long-spined black sea urchin** (*Diadema*) | Key **herbivore** controlling algae; its die-off (Caribbean, 1983) let algae smother reefs |
| **Moray eel** (Muraenidae) | Nocturnal ambush predator |
| **Parrotfish >20 cm** (Scaridae) | Scrape algae off coral; **produce sand**; vital herbivore |
| **Pencil urchin** (*Eucidaris*) | Grazer |
| **Sea cucumber** (Holothuroidea) | Deposit feeder; recycles sediment |
| **Snapper** (Lutjanidae) | Predatory fish; fishery important |
| **Demosponges** (Demospongiae) | Filter feeders; reef structure |
| **Sweetlips** (Haemulidae) | Benthic predator |
| **Triton** (*Charonia*) | Giant snail; a **natural predator of the crown-of-thorns** |

**Indo-Pacific only:** Barramundi cod (*Cromileptes altivelis*), **Bumphead parrotfish** (*Bolbometopon muricatum*, the largest parrotfish), **Giant clams** (*Tridacna*, host zooxanthellae), **Humphead (Napoleon) wrasse** (*Cheilinus undulatus*, eats crown-of-thorns).

**Atlantic only:** **Flamingo tongue snail** (*Cyphoma gibbosum*, eats gorgonians), **Gorgonia** (sea fans, octocorals), **Nassau grouper** (*Epinephelus striatus*, endangered).

> [!TIP]
> Learn each by its photo and its **role**: herbivore (controls algae), predator, cleaner, filter feeder, or coral predator/pest. Many questions ask what a species indicates about reef health.

## Part III: Water monitoring and analysis (30%)

You interpret data (no physical tests). Know each parameter, how it's measured, healthy ranges, and how they relate.

| Parameter | What it measures | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Salinity** | Dissolved salts (ppt or %) | Ocean ~35 ppt; estuaries vary; measured by hydrometer, conductivity or refractometer |
| **pH** | Acidity | Seawater ~8.1; acidification lowers it, harming shells |
| **Dissolved oxygen (DO)** | O₂ in water (mg/L) | Needed by animals; low DO (**hypoxia** < 2 mg/L) causes dead zones; **higher in cold water** |
| **Temperature** | | Controls DO solubility, metabolism, coral bleaching; thermal pollution raises it |
| **Turbidity** | Cloudiness (NTU) | From sediment/plankton; blocks light, smothers coral; measured by **Secchi disk** or turbidimeter |
| **Nitrates (NO₃⁻)** | Nitrogen nutrient | Excess from fertilizer/sewage → eutrophication |
| **Phosphates (PO₄³⁻)** | Phosphorus nutrient | Often the limiting nutrient; excess → algal blooms |
| **Fecal coliform / E. coli** | Bacteria from waste | Indicator of sewage contamination; health risk |
| **Total solids** | Dissolved + suspended solids | |
| **Biochemical oxygen demand (BOD)** | O₂ microbes use to break down organic matter | **High BOD = lots of pollution = low DO** |
| **Aragonite saturation (Ω)** | Whether water favors shell/coral building | Ω > 1 lets corals build; acidification lowers it; **Ω < 1 dissolves aragonite** |

**Relationships to understand:** warm water holds less DO; high BOD and decay lower DO; nutrients drive algal blooms that then crash and consume oxygen (eutrophication → hypoxia); turbidity and sedimentation block light and smother reefs; CO₂ lowers pH and aragonite saturation.

> [!TIP]
> A classic question: nutrients ↑ → algae bloom → algae die → bacteria decompose them (BOD ↑) → DO ↓ → fish kill. Trace the chain.

## Part IV: The salinometer (10%)

![A hydrometer floating in water](img:wq-hydrometer)

Build, calibrate and bring a **salinometer/hydrometer** that measures NaCl concentration from **1–10% (mass/volume)**, operating in a **400–600 mL beaker** with at least **400 mL** of solution. You estimate salinity to the nearest **0.1%**; full credit within **±1%** (Regional) or **±0.5%** (State/National). **No programmable or Wi-Fi devices**, no laptops or tablets.

### How it works

- **Hydrometer (density):** saltier water is **denser**, so a float rides higher. Calibrate a weighted float (a straw or tube with clay/BBs at the bottom and a paper scale inside) against known solutions. Read where the waterline hits your scale.
- **Floating/Archimedes:** the float sinks until it displaces its own weight in water; denser water supports it higher.
- **Simple electronic salinometer (allowed, non-programmable):** salt water **conducts electricity** better. A circuit with two electrodes and an LED or analog meter shows conductivity rising with salinity. Calibrate against known solutions.

### Calibration

1. Make solutions of **1%, 2%, 5%, 10%** NaCl (mass/volume: e.g., 5 g NaCl in enough water to make 100 mL for 5%).
2. Measure your device's reading in each and plot a **calibration curve** (reading vs % salinity).
3. At the event, read the unknown and use your curve to find the percent.

**Density reference** (mass/volume %, approximate):

| NaCl (% m/v) | Density (g/mL) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1.000 |
| 1 | ~1.005 |
| 2 | ~1.012 |
| 5 | ~1.034 |
| 10 | ~1.070 |

Make your float sensitive across this range (a long thin stem gives finer resolution). Bring your calibration graph.

## How to study

1. **Learn the organism list** cold (photo → common name → role in the reef). Make flashcards.
2. **Master the water parameters** and their relationships; practice reading data tables and graphs.
3. **Study estuary and reef ecology**, pollution topics, and the garbage patches.
4. **Build and calibrate your salinometer early**, and bring the calibration curve.
5. Take practice tests; add missed organisms and concepts to your notes sheet.

## Common pitfalls

- Confusing which conditions **raise** vs **lower** dissolved oxygen (cold and turbulent = more; warm, salty, high-BOD = less).
- Mixing up the Indo-Pacific-only and Atlantic-only organisms.
- Forgetting that aragonite saturation below 1 **dissolves** shells and coral.
- An uncalibrated or insensitive salinometer.
- Thinking you'll do wet chemistry tests (you only interpret data, except the salinometer).

## Test yourself

<details><summary>1. Why does high BOD lead to low dissolved oxygen?</summary>

Microbes consume oxygen as they decompose the organic matter that BOD measures.

</details>

<details><summary>2. Which organism is a natural predator of the crown-of-thorns starfish?</summary>

The triton (giant *Charonia* snail); the humphead wrasse also eats them.

</details>

<details><summary>3. What happens to coral when aragonite saturation drops below 1?</summary>

The water becomes corrosive to aragonite, so coral skeletons and shells dissolve and corals can't build.

</details>

<details><summary>4. You dissolve 5 g of NaCl to make 100 mL of solution. What's the % m/v?</summary>

5% (mass/volume).

</details>

<details><summary>5. Why are parrotfish important to reef health?</summary>

They graze algae off coral (preventing algal overgrowth) and produce sand.

</details>
