## How the event runs

You rotate through timed stations with real specimens, photos, charts and data. You **can't go back** to a station. Most stations ask you to **identify** a specimen from observable properties (and provided clues when observation isn't enough) and answer a question or two. Concept questions cover formation, classification, phase diagrams, Bowen's series, the rock cycle, uses, and (State/National) thin sections.

**Build a great binder:** a one-page property table for every specimen on the list, a dichotomous key, concept pages (silicate structures, Bowen's, phase diagrams, metamorphic facies), and a photo gallery. Tab it so you can find any mineral in seconds. Divide work with your partner: one identifies, the other looks up and answers.

## Minerals

**Definition:** a **naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and an ordered (crystalline) internal structure.** (Rocks need not be inorganic or have a fixed composition.)

### Physical properties

![Mineral properties: hardness, luster, cleavage](img:rm-properties)

| Property | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Color** | Surface color | **Unreliable** alone; trace elements change it (e.g., quartz varieties) |
| **Streak** | Color of the **powder** (rub on unglazed porcelain) | More reliable than color; hematite streaks **red-brown** even when silvery |
| **Luster** | How it reflects light | Metallic vs non-metallic (vitreous/glassy, pearly, silky, resinous, greasy, adamantine, dull/earthy) |
| **Hardness** | Resistance to scratching | **Mohs scale** (below) |
| **Cleavage** | Breaks along flat planes (planes of weak bonds) | Number and angles of planes (mica = 1, feldspar = 2 at ~90°, halite = 3 cubic, calcite = 3 rhombohedral, fluorite = 4 octahedral) |
| **Fracture** | Breaks without cleavage | Conchoidal (quartz), fibrous, hackly, uneven |
| **Density / specific gravity / heft** | Mass per volume; SG relative to water | Galena feels heavy (~7.5); most silicates ~2.6–3.0 |
| **Diaphaneity** | Transparency | Transparent, translucent, opaque |
| **Tenacity** | How it deforms | Brittle, malleable (copper, gold), sectile, flexible, elastic (mica), ductile |

**Mohs hardness scale:**

| H | Mineral | Common tester |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Talc | |
| 2 | Gypsum | Fingernail (~2.5) |
| 3 | Calcite | Copper penny (~3.5) |
| 4 | Fluorite | |
| 5 | Apatite | Glass / knife (~5.5) |
| 6 | Orthoclase | Steel file (~6.5) |
| 7 | Quartz | Scratches glass |
| 8 | Topaz | |
| 9 | Corundum | |
| 10 | Diamond | |

Mohs is **ordinal** (not linear): diamond is far harder than its "10" suggests.

### Other properties

Limited to: **reaction with acid** (calcite fizzes in HCl; dolomite fizzes only when powdered), **fluorescence** (glows under UV: some calcite, fluorite), **magnetism** (magnetite), **smell** (sulfur, some clays when wet), **taste** (halite is salty — but **no tasting at the event**), **double refraction** (calcite "Iceland spar" doubles an image), **piezoelectricity** (quartz, tourmaline produce voltage under stress), and **radioactivity** (uranium minerals).

### Crystals and structure

![The seven crystal systems](img:rm-crystal-systems)

- **Seven crystal systems:** cubic (isometric), tetragonal, orthorhombic, monoclinic, triclinic, trigonal, hexagonal. The internal lattice sets the external shape, cleavage and optical properties.
- **Mineral habit** (crystal shape): acicular (needles), bladed, botryoidal (grape-like), cubic, dendritic (branching), dodecahedral, doubly terminated, druzy (crust of tiny crystals), geodic, hexagonal, hopper, massive (no shape), micaceous (sheets), octahedral, pisolitic (pea-like), prismatic, radiating, rosette, stalactitic, twinning, tabular.

### Chemistry and classification

Minerals are grouped by their **anion or anionic group**:

| Class | Anion group | Examples (on the list) |
|---|---|---|
| **Native elements** | None (one element) | Copper, gold, silver, sulfur, diamond, graphite |
| **Sulfides** | S²⁻ | Pyrite, galena, sphalerite, chalcopyrite, bornite, stibnite |
| **Oxides / hydroxides** | O²⁻, OH⁻ | Hematite, magnetite, corundum, goethite, pyrolusite |
| **Halides** | Cl⁻, F⁻ | Halite, fluorite |
| **Carbonates** | CO₃²⁻ | Calcite, dolomite, aragonite, malachite, azurite, rhodochrosite, siderite |
| **Sulfates** | SO₄²⁻ | Gypsum, barite, celestite |
| **Borates** | BO₃ | Ulexite |
| **Silicates** | SiO₄ tetrahedra | Quartz, feldspars, micas, olivine, garnet, and most rock-forming minerals |

### Silicate structures

![Silicate tetrahedra arrangements](img:rm-silicates)

The building block is the **SiO₄ tetrahedron** (one Si surrounded by four O). How tetrahedra link sets the structure and properties:

| Structure | Linking | Group | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nesosilicates** (isolated) | Tetrahedra separate, joined by cations | | **Olivine**, garnet |
| **Sorosilicates** (island/paired) | Two tetrahedra share one oxygen | | Epidote |
| **Cyclosilicates** (ring) | Rings of tetrahedra | | Beryl, tourmaline |
| **Inosilicates** (chain) | Single chains (pyroxenes) or double chains (amphiboles) | | Augite; hornblende |
| **Phyllosilicates** (sheet) | 2D sheets → one cleavage | | Micas, clays, talc |
| **Tectosilicates** (framework) | 3D network | | **Quartz, feldspars** |

More sharing of oxygens means more Si, a more "felsic" composition, and generally lower density.

### Solid solutions and polymorphs

- **Solid solution series:** two end-members mix freely. On the list: **plagioclase feldspars** (albite ↔ anorthite) and **alkali feldspars**; **olivine** (forsterite Mg ↔ fayalite Fe); **carbonates** (calcite, siderite, rhodochrosite); **amphiboles** (actinolite ↔ tremolite); **sulfates** (barite ↔ celestite). **Feldspar ternary diagrams** plot the three feldspar end-members.
- **Polymorphs:** same composition, different structure. Examples: **diamond vs graphite** (both C), **calcite vs aragonite** (CaCO₃), **orthoclase vs microcline** (KAlSi₃O₈), and the **kyanite–andalusite–sillimanite** trio (Al₂SiO₅, each stable at different pressure/temperature).

### Phase diagrams

![An Al2SiO5 phase diagram](img:rm-phase-diagram)

- **Temperature–pressure diagrams:** show which polymorph is stable at given T and P. The classic Al₂SiO₅ diagram has three fields (kyanite at high pressure, andalusite at low pressure/low temperature, sillimanite at high temperature), meeting at a **triple point**.
- **Temperature–composition diagrams (two-component):** show melting and crystallization of a mix. A **eutectic** is the lowest-melting composition. Plagioclase's T–X diagram shows continuous solid solution (the **liquidus** and **solidus** curves).

### Formation and environments

- **Crystallization from magma** (igneous), **hydrothermal** (hot water deposits veins), **evaporites** (seawater or lake water evaporating: halite, gypsum), **chemical precipitation**, **chemical weathering** (clays from feldspar), and **metamorphic alteration** under heat and pressure.
- **Environment associations:** evaporite minerals in sedimentary basins; **mafic minerals (olivine, pyroxene)** in oceanic crust; garnet, staurolite, kyanite in metamorphic rocks.

### Bowen's Reaction Series

![Bowen's Reaction Series](img:rm-bowens)

As magma cools, minerals crystallize in order:

- **Discontinuous branch** (ferromagnesian, each mineral reacts to form the next): **olivine → pyroxene → amphibole → biotite**.
- **Continuous branch** (plagioclase feldspar, calcium-rich → sodium-rich): **anorthite → … → albite**.
- The branches meet, then **K-feldspar → muscovite → quartz** crystallize last (lowest temperature).

High-temperature minerals (olivine, Ca-plagioclase) are **mafic** and weather fastest; low-temperature minerals (quartz, K-feldspar) are **felsic** and most stable at the surface. The series explains **fractional crystallization** and why felsic magmas are the last, most evolved melts.

### Uses of minerals

Ores (galena → lead, sphalerite → zinc, hematite/magnetite → iron, chalcopyrite/bornite → copper, bauxite → aluminum), industry, construction, electronics (quartz oscillators, silicon), jewelry, geochronology (radioactive minerals), medicine. **Gemstone varieties on the list:** emerald/aquamarine/morganite (**beryl**); kunzite/hiddenite (**spodumene**); peridot (**olivine**); ruby and sapphire (**corundum**); plus pearl and amber.

## Rocks

**Definition:** a naturally occurring solid of one or more minerals (or mineraloid or organic matter). **Monomineralic** (one mineral, e.g., quartzite) or **polymineralic** (several, e.g., granite).

### The rock cycle

![The rock cycle](img:rm-rock-cycle)

Rocks change type through processes: **melting and solidification** (→ igneous), **weathering, erosion, deposition, compaction and cementation** (→ sedimentary), and **heat and pressure** causing recrystallization (→ metamorphic), plus uplift and burial. Any rock can become any other.

### Igneous rocks

![Igneous rock classification by composition and texture](img:rm-igneous)

Formed by cooling magma or lava.

**Texture** (set by cooling rate):

| Texture | Cooling | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Aphanitic** (fine-grained) | Fast, at the surface (extrusive) | Basalt, rhyolite |
| **Phaneritic** (coarse-grained) | Slow, underground (intrusive) | Granite, gabbro |
| **Glassy** | Very fast, no crystals | Obsidian |
| **Vesicular** | Trapped gas bubbles | Pumice (floats), scoria |
| **Porphyritic** | Two stages: big crystals (phenocrysts) in a fine matrix | Porphyry |
| **Pyroclastic** | Explosive fragments | Tuff |
| **Pegmatitic** | Very coarse, from water-rich late melt | Pegmatite |

**Composition:** **felsic** (light, silica-rich, quartz + K-feldspar: granite/rhyolite) → **intermediate** (diorite/andesite) → **mafic** (dark, iron/magnesium-rich: gabbro/basalt) → **ultramafic** (peridotite).

| | Intrusive (coarse) | Extrusive (fine) |
|---|---|---|
| Felsic | Granite | Rhyolite |
| Intermediate | Diorite | Andesite |
| Mafic | Gabbro | Basalt |
| Ultramafic | Peridotite | Komatiite |

**Plate tectonics:** basalt at **mid-ocean ridges**, andesite/diorite at **subduction zones**, granite at **convergent/continental** settings. **Formations:** batholith (large intrusive body), lava flow, pyroclastic deposit.

**Magma evolution:** **fractional crystallization** (minerals crystallize and settle out per Bowen's series, leaving a more felsic melt), **assimilation** (magma melts surrounding rock), **magma mixing**. **(State/National) Incompatible elements** are trace elements too big or wrongly charged to fit common mineral lattices, so they stay in the melt and concentrate in the last fluids, forming **pegmatites** (huge crystals, rare minerals, gems, economic elements like lithium and beryllium).

### Sedimentary rocks

![Sedimentary rock types](img:rm-sedimentary)

Formed at the surface from sediment or precipitation.

**Textures:**

- **Clastic (detrital):** broken fragments, named by grain size: **shale** (clay/silt), **sandstone** (sand), **conglomerate** (rounded gravel) vs **breccia** (angular gravel). **Arkose** is feldspar-rich sandstone.
- **Chemical (crystalline):** precipitated from solution: rock salt, rock gypsum, some limestone, chert.
- **Biochemical / organic:** from life: fossil limestone, chalk, coal, diatomite.

**Grain size and energy:** clay < silt < sand < pebble < cobble < boulder. **Coarse grains need high-energy water** (fast rivers, surf); **fine grains settle in low energy** (lakes, deep sea). **Sorting** (uniform vs mixed) and **rounding** (rounded = transported far; angular = nearby) reveal the environment.

**Depositional environments:** alluvial fan, delta, river (fluvial), lake (lacustrine), swamp, wind (aeolian), floodplain, beach, shallow marine (shelf), deep marine (abyssal).

**Primary structures:** plane bedding, **cross-bedding** (dunes, current direction), **ripple marks** (symmetric = waves; asymmetric = current), **mud cracks** (drying), **graded bedding** (waning current), fossil tracks and trails. These also indicate **which way was up**.

### Metamorphic rocks

![Foliated and non-foliated metamorphic rocks](img:rm-metamorphic)

Formed by heat, pressure and fluids altering a **protolith** (parent rock), without melting.

**Texture:**

- **Foliated** (layered/aligned, from directed pressure): **slate** (slaty cleavage) → **phyllite** → **schist** (visible micas) → **gneiss** (banded).
- **Non-foliated** (no alignment): **marble** (from limestone), **quartzite** (from sandstone), **hornfels**, **serpentinite** (from peridotite), **amphibolite** (from basalt/gabbro).

**Protoliths:** shale → slate/schist/gneiss; limestone → marble; sandstone → quartzite; peridotite → serpentinite; basalt or gabbro → amphibolite.

**Types:** **regional** (large-scale, from mountain building and burial) vs **contact** (local, next to an intrusion, baking the rock → hornfels).

**Grade and index minerals:** increasing metamorphism (heat/pressure) forms characteristic minerals: **chlorite (low) → muscovite → biotite → garnet → staurolite → kyanite → sillimanite (high)**.

**Metamorphic facies** (T–P conditions): zeolite, greenschist, amphibolite, granulite (increasing temperature), **blueschist and eclogite** (high pressure, low temperature: subduction zones), hornfels (contact). Read these from **T–P (pressure–temperature) diagrams**.

**Plate tectonics:** regional metamorphism at **continent–continent collisions**; **blueschist/eclogite in subduction zones**; **greenschist from basalt at ocean divergent boundaries**.

### Economic uses of rocks

Building stone (granite, marble, limestone, slate), ores, ornamental stone, agriculture (limestone for soil, gypsum), and **fossil fuels** (coal, oil shale).

### Thin sections (State and National)

![A thin section under cross-polarized light](img:rm-thin-section)

Using **photomicrographs** (photos through a polarizing microscope):

- **Optical properties in polarized light:** **twinning** (plagioclase's stripes, microcline's cross-hatch "tartan"), **extinction** (grain goes dark as the stage rotates), **cleavage planes**, **birefringence** (interference colors). Minerals to recognize: microcline, plagioclase, calcite, augite, garnet.
- **Rock textures:** igneous (**holocrystalline** fine crystalline, vesicular, glassy, porphyritic — e.g., basalt vs pumice), sedimentary (rounded vs angular, well vs poorly sorted, skeletal fragments, oolites — e.g., sandstone vs arkose).

## How to study

1. **Learn the list:** make a property card for every mineral and rock (color, luster, hardness, streak, cleavage, formula, class, uses) and every rock (type, texture, composition, protolith).
2. **Practice ID** with photos and, if you can, a real kit. Use this app's **Rocks and Minerals** practice and Photo ID modes.
3. **Learn the concept diagrams:** Bowen's series, silicate structures, the rock cycle, igneous classification chart, metamorphic facies, phase diagrams.
4. **Build and tab your binder** so you can look anything up in seconds, and bring a **field guide** and the **official list**.
5. Take timed station practice; you can't go back, so pace yourself.

## Common pitfalls

- Relying on color to ID a mineral (use streak, hardness, cleavage, habit).
- Confusing cleavage (flat planes) with crystal faces.
- Mixing up calcite (fizzes in HCl readily) and dolomite (only when powdered).
- Calling a coarse igneous rock extrusive (coarse = slow = intrusive).
- Confusing metamorphic grade minerals' order.
- Forgetting the official list, field guide and a well-tabbed binder.

## Test yourself

Hematite.

</details>

<details><summary>2. According to Bowen's series, which crystallizes first from a cooling magma: olivine or quartz?</summary>

Olivine (highest temperature); quartz crystallizes last.

</details>

<details><summary>3. A rock has large crystals and is coarse-grained. Intrusive or extrusive?</summary>

Intrusive (slow cooling underground).

</details>

<details><summary>4. What is the protolith of marble, and of quartzite?</summary>

Limestone → marble; sandstone → quartzite.

</details>

<details><summary>5. Which silicate structure gives mica its single, perfect cleavage?</summary>

Sheet silicates (phyllosilicates).

</details>

<details><summary>6. Blueschist and eclogite form in what tectonic setting?</summary>

Subduction zones (high pressure, low temperature).

</details>
