## How the event runs

You get a crime scenario, suspects, and evidence collected from the scene and from each suspect: white powders, plastic chips, fibers, hairs, ink or dye samples, fingerprints, and photos of spatters or tracks. Identify each piece, match it to suspects, then write the **analysis**: who did it, why, and **why each other suspect is eliminated**.

Speed matters: run the powder tests as a batch while your partner does polymers and fibers. Leave **at least 12–15 minutes for the written analysis**, which is 30% of the score and the tiebreaker.

## Qualitative analysis: the 15 powders

There are no mixtures, and every team gets the same set.

### The tests

| Test | How | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| **Appearance** | Look closely (hand lens) | Crystals (NaCl cubes, sugars), fine powder (starch, CaCO₃), flakes (boric acid) |
| **Water solubility** | A small amount in a few mL of water, stir | Insoluble: **CaCO₃**, **cornstarch**; slightly soluble: **CaSO₄**, **boric acid** (dissolves better hot) |
| **Iodine** | A drop on the solid | **Blue-black = starch** (cornstarch) |
| **HCl (1 M)** | A few drops on the solid | **Fizzing (CO₂) = carbonate or bicarbonate**: NaHCO₃, Na₂CO₃, CaCO₃ |
| **NaOH (1 M)** | Add to a solution, warm gently | **Ammonia smell = ammonium chloride**; white precipitate (Mg(OH)₂) with **MgSO₄**; Ca(OH)₂ may cloud with calcium salts |
| **Benedict's** | In the hot water bath | **Green → yellow → orange/red precipitate = reducing sugar: glucose**. Sucrose is **negative** (non-reducing) unless hydrolyzed first with acid |
| **Flame test** | Clean wire loop (dipped in HCl), flame | **Na: bright yellow-orange; K: lilac/violet; Li: crimson red; Ca: orange-red/brick red**; boric acid: **green**; NH₄⁺ and Mg: no characteristic color |
| **pH** (indicator paper) | Solution in water | Basic: Na₂CO₃ (~11), NaHCO₃ (~8.3), sodium acetate (~8–9); acidic: NH₄Cl (~5), boric acid (~5); neutral: NaCl, KCl, sugars |
| **Heating / charring** | Heat a sample on a flame | Sugars and starch **char** (caramel smell); NH₄Cl sublimes |
| **Smell** | Waft | **Sodium acetate** may smell of vinegar, especially with HCl added (acetic acid) |
| **Conductivity** | If provided | Ionic compounds conduct in solution; sugars don't |

### Identification table

| Compound | Formula | Water | Flame | HCl | Other key test |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium acetate | NaC₂H₃O₂ | Soluble | Yellow | Vinegar smell | Basic pH |
| Sodium chloride | NaCl | Soluble | Yellow | No reaction | Cubic crystals, neutral |
| Sodium hydrogen carbonate | NaHCO₃ | Soluble | Yellow | **Fizzes** | pH ~8 (mildly basic) |
| Sodium carbonate | Na₂CO₃ | Soluble | Yellow | **Fizzes** | pH ~11 (strongly basic) |
| Lithium chloride | LiCl | Soluble (gets warm) | **Crimson** | No | Absorbs moisture |
| Potassium chloride | KCl | Soluble | **Lilac** (view through cobalt glass if Na contaminates) | No | |
| Calcium nitrate | Ca(NO₃)₂ | Soluble | Orange-red | No | Absorbs moisture |
| Calcium sulfate | CaSO₄ | Slightly soluble | Orange-red | No | Chalky |
| Calcium carbonate | CaCO₃ | **Insoluble** | Orange-red | **Fizzes** | |
| Cornstarch | (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ | Insoluble (forms a suspension) | Burns/chars | No | **Iodine blue-black** |
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | Soluble | Chars | No | **Benedict's positive** |
| Sucrose | C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ | Soluble | Chars | No | Benedict's negative; large crystals |
| Magnesium sulfate | MgSO₄ | Soluble | None | No | **White precipitate with NaOH** |
| Boric acid | H₃BO₃ | Slightly (better hot) | **Green** | No | Slippery flakes, weakly acidic |
| Ammonium chloride | NH₄Cl | Soluble (cools the water) | None | No | **Ammonia smell with NaOH** |

**Efficient flowchart:**

1. Iodine → cornstarch.
2. HCl → fizzers (NaHCO₃, Na₂CO₃, CaCO₃). Separate with solubility (CaCO₃ insoluble) and pH or flame (Na yellow vs Ca orange-red).
3. Flame test on the rest → Li (crimson), K (lilac), B (green), Ca (orange-red: nitrate vs sulfate by solubility), Na (yellow: acetate vs chloride by smell and pH).
4. No flame color → NH₄Cl (NaOH ammonia), MgSO₄ (NaOH precipitate), sugars (charring; Benedict's separates glucose from sucrose).

## Polymers

### Plastics (no burn tests by you)

![Resin identification codes](img:for-resin-codes)

| Code | Plastic | Density (g/cm³) | Clues |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **PETE** (polyethylene terephthalate) | 1.38–1.39 | Sinks in water; clear, stiff (water bottles) |
| 2 | **HDPE** (high-density polyethylene) | 0.95–0.97 | Floats in water; waxy, opaque (milk jugs) |
| 3 | **PVC** (polyvinyl chloride) | 1.3–1.4 | Sinks; **Beilstein test** (copper wire) gives a **green flame** from chlorine |
| 4 | **LDPE** (low-density polyethylene) | 0.92–0.93 | Floats; soft and flexible (bags) |
| 5 | **PP** (polypropylene) | 0.90–0.91 | Floats, even in 0.93 solution (lowest density); stiff, hinge-friendly |
| 6 | **PS** (polystyrene, non-expanded) | 1.04–1.06 | Sinks in water, floats in salt water (~1.1); brittle, crackles |
| 7 (other) | **PMMA** (acrylic, Plexiglas) | 1.18–1.19 | Clear, glass-like |
| 7 (other) | **PC** (polycarbonate) | 1.20–1.22 | Clear, very tough |

**Density tests:** drop a sample in solutions of known density (water 1.00; alcohol–water mixtures below 1; salt or sugar solutions above 1). Remove air bubbles first. Supervisors may also provide burn data:

- **PE and PP:** blue flame with yellow tip, drips, smell like candle wax.
- **PS:** sooty yellow flame, sweet (styrene) smell.
- **PVC:** green-edged flame, hard to keep burning, acrid HCl smell.
- **PETE:** yellow, sooty, sweet smell.
- **PMMA:** blue base, fruity smell, crackles.
- **PC:** sooty, self-extinguishes.

### Fibers (burn tests allowed)

![Fiber burn tests](img:for-fibers)

| Fiber | Type | Burn behavior | Smell | Residue | Microscope |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cotton** | Plant (cellulose) | Burns fast, yellow flame, afterglow | **Burning paper** | Soft gray ash | **Flat twisted ribbon** |
| **Linen** | Plant (cellulose, flax) | Like cotton | Burning paper | Gray ash | Straight with **nodes** (like bamboo) |
| **Wool** | Animal (protein) | Burns slowly, curls away, self-extinguishes | **Burning hair** | Brittle black bead, crushable | **Scales** |
| **Silk** | Animal (protein) | Burns slowly, sputters | Burning hair | Crushable black bead | Smooth, fine, triangular cross section |
| **Nylon** | Synthetic (polyamide) | Melts, then burns, shrinks from flame | **Celery** | **Hard gray/tan bead** | Smooth, uniform rod |
| **Polyester** | Synthetic | Melts, burns with sooty smoke | **Sweet** (chemical) | **Hard black bead** | Smooth rod |
| **Spandex** | Synthetic (polyurethane) | Melts and burns, doesn't shrink away | Chemical | Soft, sticky black residue | Stretchy, fused filaments |

Rule of thumb: **plant fibers smell like paper and leave ash, animal fibers smell like hair and leave crushable beads, and synthetics melt into hard beads.**

### Hair

![Structure of a hair](img:for-hair)

- **Structure:** the **cuticle** (outer scales), the **cortex** (holds pigment), the **medulla** (central core). The **root** shows whether a hair was pulled (root intact, with tissue that can give nuclear DNA) or fell out naturally (club-shaped root).
- **Medullary index** = medulla diameter / hair diameter. **Human < 1/3**; most animals **≥ 1/2**.
- **Medulla patterns:** continuous, interrupted, fragmented, or absent (common in humans).
- **Scale patterns:** **imbricate** (flattened, overlapping: humans), **coronal** (crown-like stacked cups: bats, small rodents), **spinous** (petal-like, triangular: cats, mink, seals).

| Hair | Medulla | Scales | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Human** | Absent or fragmented; index < 1/3 | Imbricate (smooth) | Even pigment, uniform diameter |
| **Bat** | Often absent or small | **Coronal** (stacked, crown-like) | Very fine, distinctive |
| **Cow** | Often continuous or wide | Imbricate | Coarse |
| **Squirrel** | Wide, continuous; often banded color | Imbricate/irregular | |
| **Horse** | Continuous; index > 1/2 | Imbricate | Coarse, thick |

## Chromatography and spectroscopy

### Paper chromatography and TLC

![Paper chromatography of ink](img:for-chromatography)

- Spot the sample on a pencil line **above** the solvent level, let the solvent rise by capillary action, and stop before it reaches the top. Mark the **solvent front**.
- **Rf = distance traveled by the spot / distance traveled by the solvent front** (both measured from the origin line). Rf is always between 0 and 1.
- Components that are more attracted to the **mobile phase** (solvent) travel farther (higher Rf). Those more attracted to the **stationary phase** (paper or silica) stay lower.
- **Matching:** the same compound gives the same Rf **under the same conditions** (same paper, solvent, temperature). Compare scene samples to suspects run on the **same** plate.
- **TLC** (thin-layer chromatography) uses silica or alumina on glass, plastic or aluminum: faster and sharper than paper. View spots under UV if fluorescent.
- Solvents: water for water-soluble inks; isopropyl alcohol, acetone or mixtures for permanent inks.

> [!TIP]
> Example: the solvent front moved 8.0 cm and a blue spot moved 5.2 cm. Rf = 0.65.

### Mass spectra

![A mass spectrum](img:for-mass-spec)

- A mass spectrometer ionizes molecules and sorts the ions by **mass-to-charge ratio (m/z)**.
- The **molecular ion peak (M⁺)** is usually the highest-m/z significant peak: the compound's molar mass.
- The **base peak** is the tallest peak (set to 100% relative abundance).
- **Isotope patterns:** chlorine gives **M and M+2 in a 3:1 ratio**; bromine **1:1**. Carbon-13 gives a small M+1.
- **Common fragment losses:** 15 (CH₃), 17 (OH), 18 (H₂O), 28 (CO or C₂H₄), 29 (C₂H₅ or CHO), 31 (OCH₃), 45 (COOH). Common fragments: m/z 43 (C₃H₇⁺ or CH₃CO⁺), 77 (phenyl C₆H₅⁺), 91 (tropylium C₇H₇⁺).
- In Forensics, you typically **match** an unknown spectrum to reference spectra (drugs, accelerants, poisons) or identify a compound from its M⁺ and fragments.

## Crime-scene physical evidence

### Fingerprints

![The main fingerprint patterns: arch, loop, whorl](img:for-fingerprints)

**How they form:** friction ridges form in the womb (about weeks 10–17) where the **dermis** meets the **epidermis** (at the dermal papillae). They're unique (even in identical twins) and permanent. Prints are left by sweat (eccrine glands: water, amino acids, salts) and oils.

**The eight patterns:**

| Class | Pattern | Description | Frequency |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Arches** (~5%) | **Plain arch** | Ridges enter one side, rise in a gentle wave, exit the other side | |
| | **Tented arch** | A sharp spike or upthrust in the middle | |
| **Loops** (~60–65%) | **Ulnar loop** | Opens toward the **little finger** (ulna side) | Most common |
| | **Radial loop** | Opens toward the **thumb** (radius side) | |
| **Whorls** (~30–35%) | **Plain whorl** | Circular ridges with **two deltas**; a line between the deltas touches the circular ridges | |
| | **Central pocket whorl** | Two deltas; a line between them **doesn't** touch the inner circles | |
| | **Double loop whorl** | Two loops forming an S shape, two deltas | |
| | **Accidental whorl** | A combination of two or more patterns, or doesn't fit any other | |

Loops have **one delta**, arches have **none**, and whorls have **two or more**.

**Minutiae (ridge characteristics):** **ridge ending**, **bifurcation** (one ridge splits in two), **double bifurcation**, **trifurcation** (splits in three), **island** (short independent ridge, or "dot"), **enclosure** (lake: a ridge splits and rejoins), **eye** (a small enclosure), **spur** (a short branch off a ridge), **delta** (a triangular meeting point), **core** (center of a loop or whorl). Examiners compare minutiae positions to match prints.

**Development techniques:**

| Technique | Surface | Chemistry |
|---|---|---|
| **Powder dusting** | Smooth, non-porous (glass, metal) | Powder sticks to the moisture and oils |
| **Iodine fuming** | Porous (paper) | Iodine vapor dissolves in the **oils (lipids)**, staining prints yellow-brown; **temporary** (fades) |
| **Ninhydrin** | Porous (paper, cardboard) | Reacts with **amino acids** to form **Ruhemann's purple** |
| **Cyanoacrylate (superglue) fuming** | Non-porous (plastic, metal, glass) | Vapor **polymerizes** on the moisture and amino acids in the print, making white ridges (often then dyed or powdered) |
| Others | | Silver nitrate (reacts with **chloride** salts → AgCl darkens in light), DFO, small particle reagent (wet surfaces), alternate light sources |

**Types of prints:** **latent** (invisible), **patent** (visible, in blood, ink or grease), **plastic** (impressions in soft material such as wax or putty).

### DNA

![An STR electropherogram](img:for-electropherogram)

- **PCR** copies DNA: denature (~95 °C), anneal primers (~55–65 °C), extend with Taq polymerase (72 °C). Each cycle doubles the target.
- **STRs** (short tandem repeats) are short DNA sequences repeated a variable number of times at a locus. People differ in their repeat counts. The **CODIS** database uses **20 core STR loci**.
- **Electropherograms:** after PCR with fluorescent primers, **capillary electrophoresis** separates fragments by size. Each **peak** is an allele. At each locus, a person has **one peak (homozygous)** or **two peaks (heterozygous)**. **A suspect matches only if every locus matches.** A mixture (several contributors) shows more than two peaks at some loci.
- **Gel electrophoresis:** DNA is negative and moves toward the **positive** electrode; smaller fragments travel farther. Older RFLP methods compared band patterns.
- **Sources:** blood, saliva, semen, skin cells, hair with roots. **Mitochondrial DNA** (from hair shafts, bones) is inherited from the mother and is used when nuclear DNA is degraded.

### Glass

- **Refractive index (n)** = speed of light in a vacuum / speed in the material. Determine it by **immersion**: put the glass fragment in liquids of known n. When n_glass = n_liquid, the **Becke line** (a bright halo) disappears and the glass becomes nearly invisible. Moving the focus up: the Becke line moves **into the medium with higher n**.

| Glass | Typical refractive index |
|---|---|
| Vitreous silica (fused quartz) | 1.458 |
| Headlight glass | 1.47–1.49 |
| Window (float) glass | 1.51–1.52 |
| Bottle glass | 1.51–1.52 |
| Optical glass | 1.52–1.53 |
| Quartz | 1.544–1.553 |
| **Lead glass (crystal)** | 1.56–1.61 |
| Diamond (not glass) | 2.42 |

- **Density** comparisons with flotation help too.
- **Fracture analysis:** a projectile makes a **cone** (crater) that is **wider on the exit side**. **Radial fractures** spread out from the impact point; **concentric fractures** circle it. **A later fracture stops at an earlier one**, so you can order multiple impacts. **3R rule:** on a **radial** fracture's edge, the stress lines (conchoidal or rib marks) are at **right** angles to the **rear** (the side opposite the impact).

### Forensic entomology

Insects arrive on a body in a predictable **succession**, so they estimate the **postmortem interval (PMI)**.

| Stage of decay | Typical timing (warm weather) | Insects |
|---|---|---|
| **Fresh** | 0–3 days | **Blow flies** (Calliphoridae) and flesh flies arrive within minutes to hours and lay eggs |
| **Bloat** | 2–6 days | Maggots (larvae) feeding; **rove beetles** and predatory beetles (they eat maggots) |
| **Active decay** | 5–11 days | Large maggot masses, **carrion beetles**, hister beetles |
| **Advanced decay** | 10–25 days | Larvae leave to pupate; **dermestid (hide) beetles** begin |
| **Dry / remains** | 25+ days | Dermestid beetles, mites, moths (cheese skippers on dry tissue) |

**Blow fly life cycle:** egg (~1 day) → **1st, 2nd, 3rd instar larvae** (~1–2 days each, then feeding stops) → prepupa (wandering) → **pupa** (puparium, ~6–12 days) → adult. The oldest stage present gives a **minimum PMI**. Development speeds up in heat and slows in cold: forensic entomologists use **accumulated degree hours (ADH)** or degree days. Blow flies are inactive at night and below ~10 °C.

### Blood spatter

![Elliptical bloodstain showing impact angle](img:for-spatter)

- **Impact angle:** **sin θ = width / length** of the elliptical stain. A circular stain (width = length) means 90° (straight down).
- **Direction:** the **tail** (narrow end) points in the **direction of travel**.
- **Area of origin:** draw lines through the long axes of several stains back to where they converge (the **area of convergence**, in 2D). Then use each stain's impact angle: **height of origin = distance to convergence × tan θ**.
- **Velocity categories (older terminology, still common on tests):**

| Category | Speed | Drop size | Typical source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Low-velocity** (passive) | < 1.5 m/s (~5 ft/s) | ≥ 4 mm | Dripping, gravity |
| **Medium-velocity** | 1.5–7.5 m/s (5–25 ft/s) | 1–4 mm | Blunt force, stabbing, beating |
| **High-velocity** | > 30 m/s (100 ft/s) | < 1 mm (mist) | Gunshots, high-speed machinery |

Other patterns: cast-off (from a swung weapon), arterial spurts, transfer (swipes and wipes), voids (an object or person blocked the spatter), expirated (coughed blood with bubbles).

> [!TIP]
> Example: a stain is 4.0 mm wide and 8.0 mm long. sin θ = 0.50 → θ = 30°. If it lies 60 cm from the area of convergence, the origin was 60 × tan 30° ≈ 35 cm above the surface.

### Seeds and pollen

Pollen and seeds are tough, often **region-specific**, and stick to clothes and shoes. Compare **shape**, **surface texture** (spiny, smooth, net-like), and **apertures** (pores and furrows) under the microscope or in photos. A suspect with pollen from a plant that grows only at the crime scene (or only in a certain region) is linked to that place. **Forensic palynology** is the study of pollen as evidence.

### Tracks and soil

- **Footwear and tire impressions:** compare **class characteristics** (tread pattern, size, brand) and **individual characteristics** (cuts, wear, embedded stones). Class matches narrow suspects; individual characteristics can identify a single shoe or tire. Casts are made with dental stone; photos with a scale.
- **Soil:** compare **color** (Munsell chart), **texture** (sand, silt, clay %), **minerals**, **pH**, **organic content** and **density gradient** profiles. Soil on a suspect's shoes matching the scene supports (but doesn't prove) they were there.

### Blood typing and blood cells

![ABO blood typing](img:for-blood-typing)

- **ABO typing:** blood is mixed with **anti-A** and **anti-B** sera (and often **anti-Rh/D**). **Agglutination** (clumping) means the antigen is present.

| Type | Anti-A | Anti-B | Antigens | Antibodies in plasma | U.S. frequency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Clumps | No | A | anti-B | ~40% |
| **B** | No | Clumps | B | anti-A | ~11% |
| **AB** | Clumps | Clumps | A and B | Neither | ~4% |
| **O** | No | No | Neither | Both | ~45% |

Rh+ if it clumps with anti-D (~85% of people). Artificial blood kits work the same way; the supervisor provides instructions.

- **Blood cell ID under the microscope:**

| Animal | Red blood cells |
|---|---|
| **Human and other mammals** | **No nucleus**, round biconcave discs (camels and llamas: oval without a nucleus) |
| **Birds** | **Oval with a nucleus** |
| **Reptiles and amphibians** | **Oval with a nucleus**, larger (amphibians have the largest) |
| Fish | Oval with a nucleus |

Human RBCs are about 7–8 µm, smaller than most other mammals' (but larger than goats'). The **presumptive tests** for blood (Kastle–Meyer turns pink; luminol glows) and **precipitin tests** (human vs animal) may come up as concepts.

### Bullet striations

- Gun barrels have **rifling**: spiral **lands** (raised) and **grooves** that spin the bullet. Bullets pick up the **number, width and twist direction** (left or right) of lands and grooves (class characteristics) and **microscopic striations** from imperfections (individual characteristics).
- **Casings** carry **firing pin**, **breech face**, **extractor** and **ejector** marks.
- Examiners use a **comparison microscope** to line up the striations of a crime-scene bullet with a test-fired bullet. **NIBIN** is the national database of firearm evidence images.

## Writing the crime analysis (30%)

This is worth the most points and breaks ties. Structure it:

1. **Summary of each piece of evidence:** what it was identified as, and which suspect(s) it connects to.
2. **The culprit(s):** name them, list **every** piece of evidence that implicates them, and explain **why** (means, motive, opportunity if the scenario gives them).
3. **Each other suspect:** explain specifically **why they're eliminated** (or why the evidence against them is weaker or explained innocently).
4. **Answer any extra questions** the supervisor asks.

Be specific: "The white powder on the victim's desk was identified as glucose (positive Benedict's test, charred on heating), which matches the powder in Suspect 2's coat pocket. Suspect 3's powder was sucrose (negative Benedict's), so it doesn't match."

## Lab kit and notes sheet

**Kit (from the Chemistry Lab Equipment List):** well plates (spot plates), test tubes and rack, droppers, spatulas or scoops, stirring rods, beakers, wire loop for flame tests (nichrome), tongs, hand lens, pH paper, forceps, microscope slides and coverslips, ruler, wash bottle, paper towels, goggles and apron.

## Common pitfalls

- Contaminating samples: use a clean scoop and well for every test.
- Calling a Benedict's test too early (heat several minutes in the bath).
- Sodium's yellow flame masking potassium's lilac (use cobalt glass if available).
- Measuring Rf from the bottom of the paper instead of the origin line.
- Forgetting to eliminate the innocent suspects in the write-up.
- Not cleaning up (up to 10%).
