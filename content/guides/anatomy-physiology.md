## How the event runs

You and a partner get either a single test packet or a set of stations you rotate through on a timer (often 2–3 minutes each). Station tests show you a model, a slide, a diagram or a data set and ask several questions about it. Expect:

- **Identification:** label a structure on a diagram or model ("What is structure C?").
- **Function:** what a structure, cell type, hormone or enzyme does.
- **Data interpretation:** spirometry traces, flow–volume loops, oxygen–hemoglobin curves, lab values.
- **Clinical cases:** a short patient story ("a 24-year-old with bloody diarrhea and crypt abscesses…") and you name the disease, its mechanism, test or treatment.
- **Calculations:** lung volumes, minute and alveolar ventilation, compliance.

Most points come from breadth, so the best teams split the three systems, then both review the hardest one. Your one-page notes sheet should hold what you can't memorize: lung volume numbers, enzyme tables, hypersensitivity types, disease comparison tables.

## Respiratory system

![The human respiratory system](img:ap-respiratory)

### Anatomy: conducting and respiratory zones

Air moves through the **conducting zone** (nose and nasal cavity → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → terminal bronchioles), which warms, humidifies and filters it but does no gas exchange. The **respiratory zone** (respiratory bronchioles → alveolar ducts → alveolar sacs → alveoli) is where gas exchange happens. The airway branches about 23 times from trachea to alveolar sacs, and the conducting zone (about 150 mL) is **anatomical dead space**.

- **Pharynx:** nasopharynx (air only), oropharynx and laryngopharynx (air and food).
- **Larynx:** nine cartilages. Three are unpaired: thyroid (Adam's apple), cricoid (the only complete ring) and epiglottis (elastic cartilage that covers the airway when you swallow). Three are paired: arytenoid, corniculate and cuneiform. The true vocal folds make sound, and the space between them is the glottis.
- **Trachea:** 16–20 **C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage**, open at the back, where the trachealis muscle sits against the esophagus. It divides at the carina into the main bronchi. The right main bronchus is wider, shorter and more vertical, so inhaled objects usually lodge there.
- **Lungs:** right lung has 3 lobes, left has 2 plus the cardiac notch and lingula. Each lung is wrapped in visceral pleura, and parietal pleura lines the chest wall, with a thin fluid-filled pleural cavity between them.

### How the walls change from pharynx to alveoli

| Region | Epithelium | Cartilage | Smooth muscle | Glands / special cells |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nasopharynx | Pseudostratified ciliated columnar | — | — | Goblet cells |
| Oro- and laryngopharynx | Non-keratinized stratified squamous (resists abrasion from food) | — | Skeletal muscle | Mucous glands |
| Larynx | Stratified squamous on vocal folds; respiratory epithelium elsewhere | Hyaline and elastic | — | Seromucous glands |
| Trachea | Pseudostratified ciliated columnar | C-shaped hyaline rings | Trachealis | Many goblet cells and submucosal glands |
| Bronchi | Pseudostratified ciliated columnar | Irregular plates, shrinking | Spiral bands, increasing | Goblet cells, glands |
| Bronchioles (< 1 mm) | Ciliated simple columnar → cuboidal | **None** | **Most relative smooth muscle** | Club (Clara) cells replace goblet cells |
| Alveoli | Simple squamous (type I pneumocytes, ~95% of surface) | None | None | Type II pneumocytes make surfactant; alveolar macrophages ("dust cells") |

The trend to remember: going deeper, cartilage disappears, epithelium gets thinner, goblet cells vanish, and smooth muscle becomes relatively more prominent. That's why bronchiole constriction is the problem in asthma.

### Mechanics of breathing

Air flows from high to low pressure. **Boyle's law** (P₁V₁ = P₂V₂) explains how changing the chest's volume moves air.

- **Inspiration (active):** the diaphragm contracts and flattens, and the external intercostals lift the ribs. Chest volume rises, so alveolar pressure falls below atmospheric (about −1 mmHg) and air flows in. Forced inspiration adds the sternocleidomastoid, scalenes and pectoralis minor.
- **Expiration (passive at rest):** the muscles relax and the lungs' elastic recoil raises alveolar pressure above atmospheric (+1 mmHg). Forced expiration uses the internal intercostals and abdominal muscles.
- **Intrapleural pressure** is always slightly below alveolar pressure (about −4 mmHg at rest), because the lungs pull inward and the chest wall springs outward. If air gets into the pleural space (pneumothorax), the lung collapses.
- **Compliance** = ΔV / ΔP, or how easily the lungs stretch. It's lower in fibrosis (stiff lungs) and higher in emphysema (floppy lungs that recoil poorly).
- **Surfactant** (from type II cells) lowers surface tension. By the law of Laplace (P = 2T/r), small alveoli would otherwise collapse into bigger ones. Premature babies lacking surfactant develop infant respiratory distress syndrome.
- **Airway resistance:** by Poiseuille's law, resistance ∝ 1/r⁴, so halving a tube's radius raises its resistance 16×. Most resistance is in the medium-sized bronchi. Bronchioles are tiny, but there are so many in parallel that their total resistance is low. Sympathetic stimulation (β₂, epinephrine) dilates the airways, and parasympathetic stimulation (ACh, vagus) constricts them.

### Lung volumes, capacities and spirometry

![Lung volumes and capacities on a spirogram](img:ap-lung-volumes)

| Measure | Typical adult value | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Tidal volume (TV) | ~500 mL | Air per normal breath |
| Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) | ~3,000 mL | Extra air you can inhale after a normal breath |
| Expiratory reserve volume (ERV) | ~1,100 mL | Extra air you can exhale after a normal breath |
| Residual volume (RV) | ~1,200 mL | Air left after maximal exhalation (**can't be measured by simple spirometry**) |
| Inspiratory capacity (IC) | TV + IRV ≈ 3,500 mL | |
| Functional residual capacity (FRC) | ERV + RV ≈ 2,300 mL | Air in the lungs at the end of a quiet breath |
| Vital capacity (VC) | TV + IRV + ERV ≈ 4,600 mL | Maximum air you can move in one breath |
| Total lung capacity (TLC) | VC + RV ≈ 5,800 mL | |

Capacities are sums of two or more volumes. Values are roughly 20–25% lower in women.

**Ventilation formulas:**

- Minute ventilation: **V̇E = TV × respiratory rate**. Example: 500 mL × 12/min = 6 L/min.
- Alveolar ventilation: **V̇A = (TV − dead space) × rate**. Example: (500 − 150) × 12 = 4.2 L/min. Slow, deep breathing ventilates alveoli better than fast, shallow breathing at the same V̇E.

**Spirometry:** the key numbers are FEV₁ (forced expiratory volume in 1 second) and FVC (forced vital capacity).

| Pattern | FEV₁ | FVC | FEV₁/FVC | TLC | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Obstructive** (can't get air *out*) | ↓↓ | ↓ or normal | **< 0.70** | Normal or ↑ (air trapping) | Asthma, COPD, emphysema, cystic fibrosis |
| **Restrictive** (can't get air *in*) | ↓ | ↓ | Normal or ↑ | **↓** | Pulmonary fibrosis, pneumoconiosis, obesity, neuromuscular disease |

**Flow–volume loops:** a normal loop has a sharp expiratory peak and a smooth slope down. Obstruction gives a "scooped-out" (concave) expiratory curve shifted to higher lung volumes. Restriction gives a narrow loop shifted to lower volumes, with a fairly normal shape. A fixed upper-airway obstruction flattens both the inspiratory and expiratory limbs.

### Gas exchange and oxygen transport

- **Dalton's law:** each gas in a mixture exerts its own partial pressure. Air at sea level is 760 mmHg × 0.21 ≈ 160 mmHg O₂. Alveolar PO₂ is about 100–104 mmHg, because it's humidified and mixed with CO₂.
- **Henry's law:** the amount of gas that dissolves is proportional to its partial pressure. CO₂ is about 20× more soluble than O₂, which is why it diffuses so easily despite a small gradient (46 → 40 mmHg).
- **Fick's law of diffusion:** rate ∝ (surface area × partial-pressure difference) ÷ membrane thickness. Emphysema reduces surface area, and fibrosis or pulmonary edema thickens the barrier.

| | Alveoli | Arterial blood | Venous blood |
|---|---|---|---|
| PO₂ | ~104 mmHg | ~95–100 | ~40 |
| PCO₂ | ~40 | ~40 | ~45–46 |

**O₂ transport:** about 98.5% is bound to hemoglobin and about 1.5% is dissolved. Each Hb carries up to 4 O₂.

![Oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve](img:ap-o2-curve)

The **oxygen–hemoglobin dissociation curve** is sigmoid because O₂ binding is cooperative. P₅₀ (the PO₂ at 50% saturation) is about 26–27 mmHg.

- **Right shift** (lower affinity, more unloading): ↑CO₂, ↑H⁺ (↓pH, the **Bohr effect**), ↑temperature, ↑2,3-BPG. Exercising muscle creates all of these. Mnemonic: "CADET, face Right": CO₂, Acid, 2,3-DPG, Exercise, Temperature.
- **Left shift** (higher affinity): the opposites, plus fetal hemoglobin and carbon monoxide. CO binds Hb about 200× more tightly than O₂ and stops the remaining sites from unloading.

**CO₂ transport:** about 70% as bicarbonate (carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻, with the **chloride shift** as HCO₃⁻ leaves the cell), about 20–23% bound to hemoglobin (carbaminohemoglobin), and about 7–10% dissolved. In the **Haldane effect**, deoxygenated Hb carries more CO₂ and H⁺, which helps pick CO₂ up in tissues and drop it off in the lungs.

### Exercise and altitude

- **Exercise:** ventilation rises right away (neural input) and then in step with CO₂ production. Arterial PO₂ and PCO₂ stay remarkably constant in moderate exercise. Above the ventilatory (lactate) threshold, ventilation rises faster than O₂ use. Training raises VO₂max mostly by improving the heart and muscles, not the lungs.
- **High altitude:** lower atmospheric pressure means lower PO₂. Peripheral chemoreceptors trigger hyperventilation, which causes respiratory alkalosis. Acclimatization brings ↑2,3-BPG, kidney compensation (losing bicarbonate), ↑EPO and red blood cells (higher hematocrit), and more capillaries. Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction raises pulmonary pressure, and extreme altitude can cause HAPE (high-altitude pulmonary edema) and HACE (high-altitude cerebral edema).

### Respiratory disorders (all levels)

| Disorder | What goes wrong | Key features | Diagnosis / treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Asthma** | Reversible airway inflammation, bronchoconstriction and mucus; often a type I hypersensitivity | Wheezing, episodic dyspnea, triggers (allergens, exercise, cold) | Spirometry improves ≥12% after a bronchodilator; short-acting β₂ agonists (albuterol), inhaled corticosteroids |
| **COPD** | Chronic, mostly irreversible obstruction: chronic bronchitis plus emphysema | Mostly from smoking; "blue bloaters" (bronchitis) vs "pink puffers" (emphysema) | FEV₁/FVC < 0.70 after a bronchodilator; quit smoking, bronchodilators, O₂ |
| **Emphysema** | Destruction of alveolar walls, which means less surface area and recoil, plus air trapping | Barrel chest, pursed-lip breathing; centriacinar (smoking, upper lobes) vs panacinar (α₁-antitrypsin deficiency, lower lobes) | ↑TLC, ↓DLCO (gas-transfer test) |
| **Cystic fibrosis** | Autosomal recessive **CFTR** chloride-channel mutation (commonly ΔF508), giving thick mucus | Recurrent infections (*Pseudomonas*), pancreatic insufficiency, salty sweat | Sweat chloride test, newborn screening; CFTR modulators, airway clearance |
| **Pneumonia** | Infection that fills alveoli with exudate (consolidation) | Fever, productive cough, crackles; lobar vs bronchopneumonia; *S. pneumoniae* is the most common bacterial cause | Chest X-ray; antibiotics or antivirals |
| **Sleep apnea** | Repeated pauses in breathing during sleep: obstructive (airway collapses) or central (brain stops signaling) | Snoring, daytime sleepiness, obesity linked to obstructive type | Sleep study (apnea–hypopnea index); CPAP |

### State and National: control of breathing

- **Medulla:** the dorsal respiratory group (mainly inspiration) and ventral respiratory group (contains the pre-Bötzinger complex, the rhythm generator, and drives forced breathing).
- **Pons:** the pontine respiratory group (formerly "pneumotaxic center") limits inspiration and smooths the rhythm. The older "apneustic center" prolongs inspiration.
- **Central chemoreceptors** (medulla) respond to H⁺ in cerebrospinal fluid, which comes from CO₂ crossing the blood–brain barrier. **CO₂ is the main moment-to-moment driver of breathing.**
- **Peripheral chemoreceptors** (carotid bodies via the glossopharyngeal nerve, aortic bodies via the vagus) respond to low PO₂ (mostly below about 60 mmHg), high PCO₂ and low pH.
- **Hering–Breuer reflex:** stretch receptors stop over-inflation.

Additional State diseases:

| Disorder | Key facts |
|---|---|
| **Tuberculosis** | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*; caseating granulomas, Ghon complex; latent vs active disease; tested with TB skin test or interferon-γ release assay; multi-drug therapy (RIPE) |
| **Pulmonary edema** | Fluid in alveoli; cardiogenic (left heart failure) vs non-cardiogenic; crackles, frothy sputum |
| **Pleurisy** | Inflamed pleura; sharp chest pain on breathing; friction rub |
| **Pulmonary fibrosis** | Scarring of the interstitium; restrictive pattern; idiopathic form common in older adults |
| **Pneumoconioses** | Inhaled mineral dust. **Silicosis** (silica, upper lobes, eggshell calcification of lymph nodes, TB risk); **coal worker's lung** (black lung, coal macules); **asbestosis** (lower lobes, pleural plaques, raises mesothelioma and lung cancer risk) |

### National only

- **ARDS:** diffuse alveolar damage from sepsis, trauma or pneumonia, with protein-rich fluid and hyaline membranes. Severe hypoxemia that doesn't improve with O₂. Treated with low-tidal-volume ventilation and prone positioning.
- **Infant RDS:** a premature baby lacks surfactant. Prevented with antenatal corticosteroids for the mother, and treated with surfactant replacement.
- **Pulmonary hypoplasia:** underdeveloped lungs, from a congenital diaphragmatic hernia or too little amniotic fluid (oligohydramnios).
- **Small-cell lung cancer:** central, strongly linked to smoking, neuroendocrine, aggressive. Can cause paraneoplastic syndromes (SIADH, ectopic ACTH).
- **Non-small-cell lung cancer:** adenocarcinoma (most common, peripheral, the most common type in non-smokers), squamous cell (central, can raise calcium) and large cell.

## Digestive system

![The human digestive system](img:ap-digestive)

### Anatomy and spatial relationships

The **alimentary canal** runs from the mouth through the pharynx, esophagus, stomach, small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum) and large intestine (cecum and appendix, ascending, transverse, descending and sigmoid colon, rectum, anal canal). The **accessory organs** are the teeth, tongue, salivary glands (parotid, submandibular, sublingual), liver, gallbladder and pancreas.

**Layers of the anterior abdominal wall** (outside → in): skin → superficial fascia (fatty Camper's, membranous Scarpa's) → external oblique → internal oblique → transversus abdominis → transversalis fascia → extraperitoneal fat → parietal peritoneum. Rectus abdominis runs vertically inside the rectus sheath, and the midline is the linea alba.

**Peritoneum:** intraperitoneal organs hang on a mesentery and are almost fully covered by visceral peritoneum. Retroperitoneal organs sit behind the peritoneum against the back wall.

| Intraperitoneal | Retroperitoneal (mnemonic **SAD PUCKER**) |
|---|---|
| Stomach, 1st part of duodenum, jejunum, ileum, cecum, appendix, transverse and sigmoid colon, liver, gallbladder, spleen, tail of pancreas | **S**uprarenal (adrenal) glands, **A**orta and IVC, **D**uodenum (parts 2–4), **P**ancreas (head, neck, body), **U**reters, **C**olon (ascending and descending), **K**idneys, **E**sophagus (lower part), **R**ectum (lower part) |

### The four layers of the gut wall

![Layers of the gastrointestinal wall](img:ap-gut-wall)

1. **Mucosa:** epithelium, lamina propria (loose connective tissue with blood vessels and lymphoid tissue), and muscularis mucosae (a thin smooth muscle layer).
2. **Submucosa:** dense connective tissue, larger vessels, glands (Brunner's glands in the duodenum), and the **submucosal (Meissner's) plexus**, which controls secretion.
3. **Muscularis externa:** inner circular and outer longitudinal smooth muscle, with the **myenteric (Auerbach's) plexus** between them controlling motility. The stomach adds an inner oblique layer, the upper esophagus is skeletal muscle, and the colon's longitudinal layer gathers into three bands (taeniae coli).
4. **Serosa** (visceral peritoneum) or **adventitia** (fibrous tissue where the organ isn't in the peritoneum, such as most of the esophagus).

### Comparing the linings

| Organ | Epithelium | Special features |
|---|---|---|
| Esophagus | Non-keratinized stratified squamous | Upper and lower sphincters; mucous glands |
| Stomach | Simple columnar (all mucus-secreting) | Rugae, gastric pits and glands: **parietal cells** (HCl, intrinsic factor), **chief cells** (pepsinogen, gastric lipase), **G cells** (gastrin), mucous neck cells, enteroendocrine cells |
| Small intestine | Simple columnar with **microvilli** (brush border) | Folds (plicae circulares) → **villi** → microvilli multiply surface area; crypts of Lieberkühn; goblet cells; **Paneth cells** (antimicrobials); Brunner's glands (duodenum, alkaline mucus); **Peyer's patches** (ileum) |
| Large intestine | Simple columnar with **many goblet cells** | No villi; deep straight crypts; haustra, taeniae coli; absorbs water and electrolytes |

### Motility and its control

- **Peristalsis:** waves that push food forward. **Segmentation:** back-and-forth mixing contractions, mostly in the small intestine. The **migrating motor complex** sweeps the gut between meals and is triggered by motilin.
- **Swallowing:** a voluntary oral phase, then reflex pharyngeal and esophageal phases. The epiglottis covers the larynx and the soft palate closes the nasopharynx.
- **Gastric emptying** is slowed by fat and acid entering the duodenum (via CCK and the enterogastric reflex).
- **Enteric nervous system:** the "second brain," which runs reflexes on its own and is modulated by the parasympathetic system (vagus, mostly stimulates digestion) and sympathetic system (mostly inhibits it).

| Hormone | Made by | Trigger | Main actions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gastrin** | G cells (stomach antrum) | Peptides, stomach stretch, vagus | ↑ HCl, ↑ gastric motility, mucosal growth |
| **Secretin** | S cells (duodenum) | Acid in duodenum | ↑ pancreatic **bicarbonate**, ↓ gastric acid |
| **CCK** | I cells (duodenum, jejunum) | Fats, amino acids | Gallbladder contracts, ↑ pancreatic **enzymes**, slows gastric emptying, satiety |
| **GIP** | K cells (duodenum) | Glucose, fat | ↑ insulin release, ↓ acid |
| **Motilin** | M cells (small intestine) | Fasting | Starts the migrating motor complex |
| **Ghrelin** | Stomach | Fasting | Hunger |
| **Somatostatin** | D cells | Acid | Inhibits most GI hormones and secretions |

Gastric secretion has three phases: **cephalic** (sight, smell or thought of food, via the vagus), **gastric** (stretch and peptides in the stomach, via gastrin) and **intestinal** (chyme in the duodenum, mostly inhibitory).

### Mechanical vs chemical digestion

Mechanical digestion (chewing, churning, segmentation) breaks food into smaller pieces. Chemical digestion is enzymatic hydrolysis of bonds.

| Nutrient | Mouth | Stomach | Small intestine (pancreatic) | Brush border | Absorbed as |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | Salivary amylase | (amylase is inactivated by acid) | Pancreatic amylase → maltose, maltotriose, α-dextrins | Maltase, sucrase-isomaltase, **lactase** | Glucose and galactose (SGLT1, with Na⁺), fructose (GLUT5); all leave via GLUT2 |
| **Proteins** | — | **Pepsin** (pepsinogen activated by HCl) | Trypsin (trypsinogen activated by **enteropeptidase**; trypsin then activates the rest), chymotrypsin, elastase, carboxypeptidase | Aminopeptidases, dipeptidases | Amino acids, di- and tripeptides (PepT1) |
| **Fats** | Lingual lipase (minor) | Gastric lipase (minor) | **Bile salts emulsify**; pancreatic lipase + colipase → monoglycerides + free fatty acids | — | Micelles → enterocytes re-form triglycerides → **chylomicrons** → lacteals (lymph) |

### Absorption

- Most absorption happens in the **small intestine**. About 9 L of fluid enters the gut daily (2 L from food and drink, 7 L from secretions), and only about 100–200 mL leaves in stool.
- **Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K)** are absorbed with fat in micelles, so fat malabsorption causes deficiencies. **B₁₂** needs intrinsic factor and is absorbed in the **terminal ileum**. **Iron** (as Fe²⁺, via DMT1) and **calcium** (helped by vitamin D) are absorbed mainly in the **duodenum**.
- **Fiber:** soluble fiber (oats, pectin) forms a gel, slows glucose absorption, lowers LDL and feeds colonic bacteria. Insoluble fiber (cellulose, bran) adds bulk and speeds transit.

### Exercise and obesity

- **Exercise:** intense exercise diverts blood from the gut to muscle (sympathetic vasoconstriction), slowing digestion and sometimes causing cramps or GI symptoms in endurance athletes. Regular moderate exercise improves motility and lowers colon cancer and diverticular disease risk.
- **Obesity:** raises the risk of GERD (higher abdominal pressure), gallstones, MASLD, pancreatitis and colorectal cancer. It's also linked to changes in the gut microbiome and to insulin resistance.

### State and National: liver, pancreas and more

![Liver lobule histology](img:ap-liver-lobule)

- **Liver lobule:** hexagonal, with a **central vein** in the middle and **portal triads** (hepatic artery, portal vein, bile duct) at the corners. Blood flows through the sinusoids from the triads to the central vein, and bile flows the opposite way. Key cells: hepatocytes, **Kupffer cells** (macrophages lining sinusoids), **stellate (Ito) cells** (store vitamin A, cause scarring in cirrhosis), and endothelial cells.
- **Pancreas:** the exocrine part (acinar cells make enzymes, duct cells make bicarbonate) and the endocrine **islets of Langerhans**: α cells (glucagon), β cells (insulin), δ cells (somatostatin), PP cells.
- **Glucose homeostasis:** after a meal, insulin drives glucose uptake (GLUT4) and glycogen and fat synthesis. Fasting brings glucagon, so glycogenolysis and then gluconeogenesis in the liver.
- **Cholesterol:** the liver makes cholesterol (HMG-CoA reductase, the statin target) and bile acids. **LDL** carries cholesterol to tissues (high LDL means atherosclerosis), and **HDL** carries it back to the liver ("good").
- **Metabolic syndrome:** central obesity, high triglycerides, low HDL, high blood pressure and high fasting glucose. It drives MASLD and type 2 diabetes.
- **Toxins:** **acetaminophen** overdose saturates normal metabolism, producing NAPQI (via CYP2E1) that depletes glutathione and kills hepatocytes around the central vein (antidote: N-acetylcysteine). **Alcohol:** fatty liver → alcoholic hepatitis → cirrhosis. **Anabolic steroids:** cholestasis, blood-filled cysts (peliosis hepatis), liver adenomas. **Heavy metals** (lead, mercury, cadmium, arsenic): oxidative damage. **Bisphenols (BPA)** and **PCBs:** endocrine disruptors linked to fatty liver and metabolic dysfunction.
- **Gut microbiome:** ferments fiber into short-chain fatty acids (butyrate feeds colon cells), makes vitamin K and some B vitamins, trains the immune system, and blocks pathogens by competing with them (colonization resistance). Antibiotics can upset it and let *C. difficile* take over.
- **Diseases:** **pancreatitis** (gallstones and alcohol are the top causes; pain radiating to the back; ↑ lipase), **MASLD** (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, formerly NAFLD), **cirrhosis** (fibrosis and regenerative nodules, leading to portal hypertension, varices, ascites and encephalopathy), **hepatitis** (A and E: fecal–oral, acute; B, C and D: blood-borne, can become chronic; C often has no symptoms), and **cancers** (colorectal, from polyps; gastric, linked to *H. pylori*; hepatocellular, from cirrhosis or HBV/HCV).

### National only

- **Achalasia:** the myenteric plexus is lost, so the lower esophageal sphincter can't relax. Trouble swallowing solids and liquids; "bird's beak" on barium swallow.
- **Hirschsprung disease:** neural crest cells fail to migrate, leaving no ganglion cells in the distal colon. Delayed meconium in newborns and a distended bowel.
- **Biliary disorders:** cholecystitis (inflamed gallbladder, usually from a stone; Murphy's sign); **choledocholithiasis** (stone in the common bile duct); **ascending cholangitis** (infected obstructed duct; Charcot's triad of fever, jaundice and right-upper-quadrant pain); **Mirizzi syndrome** (gallbladder-neck stone presses on the common hepatic duct); **PBC** (autoimmune destruction of small intrahepatic ducts, mostly in women, anti-mitochondrial antibodies); **PSC** (scarring of large ducts, "beaded" ducts, linked to ulcerative colitis); **Gilbert syndrome** (mild unconjugated bilirubin rise during stress or fasting, harmless); **Crigler–Najjar** (severe UGT1A1 deficiency, risk of kernicterus).
- **Cancers:** esophageal (squamous, linked to smoking and alcohol; adenocarcinoma, linked to Barrett's), pancreatic (often found late; CA 19-9; Whipple procedure) and neuroendocrine tumors (carcinoid syndrome).

## Immune system

### Organs

- **Primary lymphoid organs** (where lymphocytes mature): **bone marrow** (B cells mature there; all blood cells start there) and **thymus** (T cells mature there; it shrinks after puberty).
- **Secondary lymphoid organs** (where immune responses start): **lymph nodes** (B cells in cortical follicles, T cells in the paracortex, macrophages in the medullary sinuses), **spleen** (red pulp filters old red blood cells; white pulp is lymphoid tissue for blood-borne antigens), and mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (tonsils, Peyer's patches).
- **Skin:** a physical barrier with acidic pH, antimicrobial peptides, normal flora and Langerhans cells (dendritic cells).

### Innate immunity: fast and non-specific

- **Barriers:** skin, mucus, cilia, stomach acid, lysozyme (in tears and saliva), defensins.
- **Complement:** three pathways (classical via antibody, lectin via mannose-binding lectin, alternative via spontaneous C3 activation) that all meet at **C3**. Results: **C3b** opsonization, **C3a/C5a** inflammation and recruitment (C5a is a chemoattractant), and the **membrane attack complex (C5b–C9)** that punches holes in cells.
- **Pattern recognition:** **Toll-like receptors** recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns. TLR4 recognizes LPS (Gram-negative bacteria), TLR3 double-stranded RNA, TLR5 flagellin, TLR9 unmethylated CpG DNA. Signaling activates NF-κB and inflammatory cytokines.
- **Inflammation:** the five signs are redness, heat, swelling, pain and loss of function. Histamine and prostaglandins dilate vessels and make them leaky, and neutrophils arrive first.
- **Fever:** IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-α make the hypothalamus produce prostaglandin E₂, which raises the body's set point.
- **Cells:** neutrophils (most abundant white blood cells, first responders), macrophages (phagocytes that present antigen), dendritic cells (the best antigen-presenting cells, linking innate and adaptive immunity), NK cells (kill cells missing MHC I), eosinophils (parasites, allergy), basophils and mast cells (histamine, IgE-mediated).

### Adaptive immunity: slow first time, specific, with memory

- **B cells** make antibodies (humoral immunity). **T cells:** CD4⁺ helpers (Th1 activates macrophages, Th2 helps B cells and fights parasites, Th17 drives neutrophil inflammation, Tregs suppress) and CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells (kill infected or cancer cells via perforin and granzymes).
- **Antigen presentation:** **MHC class I** is on all nucleated cells and shows peptides from inside the cell to CD8⁺ T cells. **MHC class II** is on antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages, B cells) and shows peptides from outside the cell to CD4⁺ T cells.
- **Diversity:** V(D)J recombination of receptor genes (RAG1/RAG2) creates millions of different receptors.
- **T-cell education in the thymus:** **positive selection** in the cortex keeps cells that can bind self-MHC, and **negative selection** in the medulla (helped by AIRE) deletes cells that strongly bind self-antigens. That's **central tolerance**. **Peripheral tolerance** adds Tregs and anergy.
- **Activation** needs two signals: antigen–MHC plus co-stimulation (B7 on the antigen-presenting cell binding CD28 on the T cell). Without signal 2, the T cell becomes unresponsive (anergic).
- **Clonal selection and expansion → effector cells and memory cells.** B cells in germinal centers undergo **somatic hypermutation** (affinity maturation) and **class switching**.
- **Primary vs secondary response:** the first exposure has a lag (about 7–10 days) and makes mostly IgM. A second exposure is faster and bigger, making mostly high-affinity IgG.

### Antibodies

![Antibody structure](img:ap-antibody)

Each antibody (immunoglobulin) has two heavy and two light chains joined by disulfide bonds. The **Fab** arms (variable regions) bind antigen, and the **Fc** stem (constant region) decides the antibody's class and its job (binding Fc receptors, activating complement).

| Class | Structure | Share of serum Ig | Key facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **IgG** | Monomer | ~75–80% | Most abundant; **crosses the placenta**; opsonizes, activates complement, neutralizes; main secondary-response antibody; 4 subclasses (IgG1–4) |
| **IgM** | **Pentamer** (with J chain) | ~5–10% | First antibody made; best at activating complement; B-cell receptor (as a monomer) |
| **IgA** | Dimer in secretions (secretory component), monomer in serum | ~10–15% | Mucosal immunity: tears, saliva, breast milk, gut; 2 subclasses (IgA1, IgA2) |
| **IgD** | Monomer | < 1% | On naive B cells with IgM; role in B-cell activation |
| **IgE** | Monomer | Trace | Binds mast cells and basophils; allergies (type I) and parasites |

### Active vs passive immunity

| | Active (your body makes antibodies; long-lasting memory) | Passive (you receive antibodies; immediate but temporary) |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural** | Getting infected | Maternal IgG across the placenta, IgA in breast milk |
| **Artificial** | **Vaccination** | Antibody injections (antivenom, monoclonal antibodies, immune globulin) |

**Vaccine types:** live attenuated (MMR, varicella; strong immunity, not for immunocompromised people), inactivated (polio IPV, influenza shot), subunit, recombinant or conjugate (hepatitis B, HPV, Hib, pneumococcal), toxoid (tetanus, diphtheria), mRNA (COVID-19), viral vector. **Herd immunity** protects people who can't be vaccinated.

### Immune disorders (all levels)

- **Immunodeficiencies:** **HIV/AIDS** (HIV uses CD4 and CCR5/CXCR4 to infect CD4⁺ T cells; AIDS is a CD4 count below 200/µL or an AIDS-defining illness; treated with antiretroviral therapy), **CVID** (common variable immunodeficiency: B cells fail to become plasma cells, giving low antibodies and recurrent sinus and lung infections, often diagnosed in adults), **SCID** (no functional T cells and so no working B cells either; X-linked IL-2Rγ chain or ADA deficiency; "bubble boy"; treated with bone marrow transplant).
- **Autoimmune:** **multiple sclerosis** (T cells attack CNS myelin; relapsing–remitting; MRI plaques), **rheumatoid arthritis** (symmetric small-joint arthritis, pannus, rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibodies), **Hashimoto's thyroiditis** (anti-TPO antibodies, the most common cause of hypothyroidism), **Graves' disease** (antibodies that stimulate the TSH receptor cause hyperthyroidism and bulging eyes), **Sjögren's syndrome** (attacks salivary and tear glands, giving dry eyes and mouth; anti-Ro/La antibodies).

| Hypersensitivity type | Mechanism | Rules' examples |
|---|---|---|
| **I: immediate** | IgE on mast cells; histamine release within minutes | Allergic **asthma**, **anaphylaxis**, **urticaria** (hives) |
| **II: antibody-mediated (cytotoxic)** | IgG or IgM against cell-surface antigens | **Myasthenia gravis** (antibodies block ACh receptors) |
| **III: immune complex** | Antigen–antibody complexes deposit and activate complement | **Arthus reaction** (local), **serum sickness** (systemic) |
| **IV: delayed (T-cell)** | T cells and macrophages, 48–72 hours | **Contact dermatitis** (poison ivy, nickel), **celiac disease** (gluten-specific T cells) |

### State and National

- **Signaling:** cytokines (IL-2 for T-cell growth, IL-4 for Th2 and IgE, IL-6, IL-10 and TGF-β suppress, TNF-α, interferons). Type I interferons (α, β) fight viruses, and IFN-γ activates macrophages. **JAK-STAT:** cytokine receptors activate JAKs, which phosphorylate STATs that enter the nucleus and switch on genes (JAK inhibitors treat RA). **cGAS-STING:** cGAS senses DNA in the cytoplasm, makes cGAMP, which activates STING and leads to type I interferon.
- **Antibody detail:** IgG1–4 differ in hinge and complement activation (IgG3 is the strongest complement activator and IgG4 the weakest; IgG2 responds to polysaccharides). IgA1 vs IgA2 differ in hinge length (IgA1 is susceptible to bacterial proteases).
- **Transplants:** autograft (from yourself), isograft (identical twin), allograft (same species), xenograft (another species). Rejection: **hyperacute** (minutes, pre-formed antibodies such as ABO), **acute** (days to weeks, T cells), **chronic** (months to years, vessel scarring). Prevented with HLA matching and immunosuppressants (calcineurin inhibitors). **Graft-versus-host disease:** donor T cells attack the host after a bone marrow transplant (skin, liver, gut).
- **More diseases:** type 1 diabetes (T cells destroy β cells), lupus (anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith antibodies; malar rash; type III), dermatomyositis (muscle weakness plus heliotrope rash), scleroderma (fibrosis; CREST), psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis (Th17, IL-17/23), erythroblastosis fetalis (Rh⁻ mother's IgG attacks an Rh⁺ fetus; prevented with RhoGAM), DiGeorge (22q11 deletion, no thymus), Bruton's (X-linked BTK, no B cells), Job's (hyper-IgE, STAT3), Bloom's (BLM helicase, DNA repair), Wiskott–Aldrich (eczema, low platelets, infections), MHC I/II deficiency (bare lymphocyte syndrome).

### National only

- **Tumor immunology:** immune surveillance (NK cells and CD8⁺ T cells), tumors escaping it (losing MHC I, PD-L1, regulatory T cells). Therapies: cancer vaccines, cytokines (IL-2, interferon-α), antibodies (checkpoint inhibitors such as anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4; rituximab against CD20), **CAR-T** (a patient's T cells engineered to target, for example, CD19).
- **Vaccine routes:** intramuscular, subcutaneous, intradermal, oral, intranasal (mucosal vaccines build IgA). **Adjuvants** (alum, oil-in-water emulsions, TLR agonists) boost the innate response.
- **Immunological imprinting** ("original antigenic sin"): the first strain you meet shapes responses to later ones. **Antibody-dependent enhancement:** non-neutralizing antibodies help a virus enter cells (dengue).
- **Leukemias:** ALL (children), AML, CLL (older adults), CML (Philadelphia chromosome, BCR-ABL, imatinib). **Neutropenias:** severe congenital (ELANE mutations) and cyclic (about 21-day cycles). **Superantigens:** staph enterotoxin food poisoning and toxic shock syndrome (they cross-link MHC II and T-cell receptors, causing a massive cytokine release).

## How to study

1. **Weeks 1–2:** learn anatomy with labeled diagrams (OpenStax Anatomy & Physiology chapters 22–24 and 21 are free and match this event). Draw each system from memory.
2. **Weeks 3–4:** physiology. Work every formula (ventilation, compliance), the O₂ curve shifts, enzyme tables and hormone tables.
3. **Weeks 5–6:** diseases. Make one comparison table per system: cause, mechanism, signs, diagnosis, treatment.
4. **From week 6:** timed practice tests and building your notes sheet. Every missed question goes on a "fix list."

**The notes sheet:** keep it to what you can't memorize. Lung volume numbers and capacity sums, the ODC shift list, the digestive enzyme table, the hormone table, SAD PUCKER, the antibody class table, the hypersensitivity table, UC vs Crohn's, and the State/National disease lists. Use tiny tables, abbreviations and both sides.

## Common pitfalls

- Confusing **capacities** (sums) with **volumes**. RV, FRC and TLC can't come from simple spirometry.
- Getting O₂-curve shifts backwards. Right = **R**eleases O₂.
- Mixing up which pancreatic hormones control **bicarbonate** (secretin) and **enzymes** (CCK).
- Calling Crohn's continuous (it's **skip lesions** and **transmural**); ulcerative colitis is continuous and limited to the mucosa.
- Forgetting that myasthenia gravis and Graves' are **type II**, and contact dermatitis is **type IV**.
- At stations, misreading what is labeled. Check where the line actually points.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>1. A patient's FEV₁/FVC is 0.55 and TLC is 120% of predicted. Obstructive or restrictive? Name a likely disease.</summary>

Obstructive (low ratio, air trapping raises TLC). Emphysema or COPD is the most likely cause.

</details>

<details><summary>2. Tidal volume 400 mL, dead space 150 mL, rate 20/min. Find minute and alveolar ventilation.</summary>

Minute ventilation = 400 × 20 = 8.0 L/min. Alveolar ventilation = (400 − 150) × 20 = 5.0 L/min.

</details>

<details><summary>3. Which way does the O₂–Hb curve shift in an exercising muscle, and why does that help?</summary>

To the right (↑CO₂, ↑H⁺, ↑temperature). Hemoglobin releases more O₂ to the working muscle.

</details>

<details><summary>4. Which hormone stimulates pancreatic bicarbonate, and what triggers it?</summary>

Secretin, from duodenal S cells, released when acid enters the duodenum.

</details>

<details><summary>5. Name the four layers of the GI wall and where each nerve plexus sits.</summary>

Mucosa, submucosa (Meissner's plexus), muscularis externa (Auerbach's myenteric plexus between the circular and longitudinal layers), serosa or adventitia.

</details>

<details><summary>6. Which antibody crosses the placenta, and which is first made in a primary response?</summary>

IgG crosses the placenta. IgM is made first.

</details>

<details><summary>7. Classify: poison ivy rash, serum sickness, anaphylaxis, myasthenia gravis.</summary>

Type IV, type III, type I, type II.

</details>

<details><summary>8. Where is B₁₂ absorbed, and what does it need?</summary>

The terminal ileum, with intrinsic factor from the stomach's parietal cells.

</details>
