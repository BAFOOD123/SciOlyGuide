## How the event runs

The rules list a broad scope: anatomy (cells, roots, stems, leaves, spore-forming bodies, flowers), growth and transport, the major groups of plants, and fifteen extra topics from the history of botany to GMOs. Supervisors pick from all of it, so **breadth wins**.

Station tests often put a slide under a microscope ("Is this a monocot or dicot stem? Label the xylem."), a real plant or flower ("Count the stamens; what family?") or a graph ("Which curve is a C₄ plant?"). Written tests lean on physiology and concepts.

Split the scope with your partner: one takes **structure and diversity** (anatomy, life cycles, taxonomy, evolution), the other takes **physiology and applied botany** (photosynthesis, hormones, nutrition, diseases, agriculture, medicine). Both need the plant cell and photosynthesis.

## Plant cells and tissues

![Diagram of a plant cell](img:bot-plant-cell)

**What makes plant cells different from animal cells:**

- **Cell wall** of cellulose (primary wall), with lignin in secondary walls of woody cells. Cells connect through **plasmodesmata**. The **middle lamella** (pectin) glues neighbor cells.
- **Chloroplasts** (photosynthesis) and other plastids: amyloplasts (store starch), chromoplasts (pigments in fruit and petals).
- A large **central vacuole** (tonoplast membrane) holding water, ions and pigments, keeping the cell turgid.

**Three tissue systems:**

| System | What it does | Made of |
|---|---|---|
| **Dermal** | Covers and protects | Epidermis (with a waxy **cuticle**, **stomata** flanked by guard cells, trichomes, root hairs); **periderm** (cork) in woody plants |
| **Vascular** | Transports | **Xylem** (water and minerals upward; dead cells: **tracheids** and **vessel elements**) and **phloem** (sugars in both directions; living **sieve-tube elements** with **companion cells**) |
| **Ground** | Everything else: photosynthesis, storage, support | **Parenchyma** (thin walls, alive, the generalist), **collenchyma** (unevenly thickened walls, flexible support in growing parts, e.g. celery strings), **sclerenchyma** (thick lignified walls, often dead: **fibers** and **sclereids**, the grit in pears) |

**Meristems** are where growth happens:

- **Apical meristems** at root and shoot tips drive **primary growth** (length).
- **Lateral meristems** drive **secondary growth** (girth). The **vascular cambium** makes secondary xylem (wood) inward and secondary phloem outward. The **cork cambium** makes the periderm.
- **Intercalary meristems** at grass nodes let lawns regrow after mowing.

## Roots, stems and leaves

### Roots

- **Taproot** (one main root; most dicots: carrots) vs **fibrous roots** (many thin roots; monocots: grasses). Adventitious roots grow from stems (corn prop roots).
- **Zones from the tip up:** root cap (protects, senses gravity with statoliths) → zone of cell division → zone of elongation → zone of maturation (root hairs).
- **Cross section from outside in:** epidermis → **cortex** (storage) → **endodermis** with the waxy **Casparian strip** (forces water to cross a membrane, so the plant controls what gets in) → pericycle (where lateral roots form) → vascular cylinder (stele).
- Dicot roots have xylem in an X or star shape. Monocot roots have a ring of xylem and phloem around a central pith.
- **Special roots:** storage (sweet potato), prop and buttress roots, pneumatophores (mangroves breathing), aerial roots (orchids), and haustoria (parasitic plants).
- **Partnerships:** **mycorrhizae** (fungi trading phosphorus and water for sugar; ~80–90% of plants) and **root nodules** with nitrogen-fixing *Rhizobium* (legumes).

### Stems

![Cross sections of monocot and dicot stems](img:bot-stem-xs)

- **Nodes** (where leaves attach) and **internodes**. **Axillary buds** sit in the angle between leaf and stem. **Apical dominance**: the terminal bud's auxin suppresses side buds, which is why pinching the tip makes a plant bushier.
- **Dicot stem:** vascular bundles in a **ring**, with pith in the center and cortex outside. **Monocot stem:** bundles **scattered** through ground tissue.
- **Wood:** each year the cambium makes large thin-walled **spring (early) wood** and dense **summer (late) wood**, forming annual rings (dendrochronology dates wood by them). **Heartwood** is old, dark, non-conducting xylem. **Sapwood** is younger, active xylem. "Bark" is everything outside the vascular cambium: secondary phloem plus periderm.
- **Modified stems:** rhizomes (ginger, iris), stolons/runners (strawberry), tubers (potato; the "eyes" are buds), bulbs (onion; mostly fleshy leaves on a short stem), corms (crocus), tendrils (grape), thorns (hawthorn), cladodes (cactus pads).

### Leaves

![Cross section of a leaf](img:bot-leaf-xs)

- **Parts:** blade, petiole (stalk), stipules. **Simple** (one blade) vs **compound** (leaflets: pinnate or palmate). **Arrangement:** alternate, opposite, whorled. **Venation:** net or reticulate (dicots) vs parallel (monocots).
- **Cross section (top to bottom):** cuticle → upper epidermis → **palisade mesophyll** (column cells packed with chloroplasts, most photosynthesis) → **spongy mesophyll** (loose, with air spaces for gas exchange) → lower epidermis with most **stomata**. Veins hold xylem on top and phloem underneath, often in a bundle sheath.
- **Guard cells** open stomata when they take up K⁺ and water and swell (triggered by light and low CO₂). **Abscisic acid** closes them in drought.
- **Modified leaves:** spines (cactus), tendrils (pea), bracts (poinsettia's red "petals"), succulent storage leaves (aloe), and insect traps (Venus flytrap, pitcher plants, sundews).
- **Adaptations:** sun leaves are thick and small; shade leaves are thin and broad. Xerophytes have sunken stomata, thick cuticles and hairs. Hydrophytes have air spaces (aerenchyma) and stomata on top (water lilies).

## Transport

- **Water (xylem):** **cohesion–tension theory**. Transpiration from leaves pulls a continuous column of water up, held together by **cohesion** (hydrogen bonds) and to vessel walls by **adhesion**. Water moves from high to low **water potential** (Ψ = Ψs + Ψp, solute plus pressure potential): soil → root → stem → leaf → air. Root pressure contributes a little (guttation drops at leaf tips at night).
- **Paths through the root:** apoplast (through cell walls and spaces), symplast (through cytoplasm via plasmodesmata) and transmembrane. The Casparian strip blocks the apoplast at the endodermis.
- **Transpiration** rises with light, heat, wind and low humidity, and falls when stomata close. A **potometer** measures it.
- **Sugar (phloem):** the **pressure-flow (mass flow) hypothesis**. At a **source** (leaf), sugar is loaded into sieve tubes, water follows by osmosis and pressure builds. At a **sink** (root, fruit, growing tip), sugar is unloaded and pressure drops, so sap flows from source to sink. Sucrose is the main transported sugar.

## Photosynthesis

![Light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membrane](img:bot-photosynthesis)

**6CO₂ + 6H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂**

| | Light-dependent reactions | Calvin cycle (light-independent) |
|---|---|---|
| Where | **Thylakoid** membranes | **Stroma** |
| Inputs | Light, H₂O, NADP⁺, ADP | CO₂, ATP, NADPH |
| Outputs | O₂ (from **water**), ATP, NADPH | G3P (makes glucose, sucrose, starch) |
| Key parts | **Photosystem II** (P680) splits water → electron transport chain (plastoquinone, cytochrome b₆f, plastocyanin) → **Photosystem I** (P700) → ferredoxin → NADP⁺ reductase; **ATP synthase** driven by the H⁺ gradient (chemiosmosis) | **RuBisCO** fixes CO₂ onto RuBP (5C) → two 3-PGA → reduced to G3P; 3 turns of the cycle make 1 net G3P (using 9 ATP and 6 NADPH) |

- **Pigments:** chlorophyll a (main pigment, absorbs blue and red), chlorophyll b and **carotenoids** (accessory pigments that widen the absorbed range and protect against excess light). Leaves look green because green light is reflected. The **absorption spectrum** of chlorophyll vs the **action spectrum** of photosynthesis (Engelmann's experiment with algae and bacteria) is a classic graph question.
- **Photorespiration:** RuBisCO also binds O₂, which wastes energy, especially when it's hot and stomata are closed.

| | C₃ | C₄ | CAM |
|---|---|---|---|
| First product | 3-PGA (3 carbons) | Oxaloacetate (4 carbons) | Oxaloacetate/malate (4 carbons) |
| How they avoid photorespiration | They don't | **Separate in space**: PEP carboxylase fixes CO₂ in mesophyll cells, then CO₂ is released to RuBisCO in **bundle sheath** cells (**Kranz anatomy**) | **Separate in time**: stomata open at **night**, CO₂ stored as malic acid in vacuoles, used by day |
| Examples | Wheat, rice, soybeans, most trees | **Corn, sugarcane**, sorghum, crabgrass | Cacti, pineapple, jade plant, agave |

**Limiting factors:** light intensity, CO₂ concentration and temperature. Rates rise with light until the **light saturation point**; the **light compensation point** is where photosynthesis equals respiration.

**Cellular respiration** happens in plants too (in mitochondria, all the time): glycolysis → Krebs cycle → electron transport, making ATP from sugar.

## Plant hormones and responses

| Hormone | Main effects |
|---|---|
| **Auxin** (IAA) | Cell elongation, **phototropism** and **gravitropism** (moves to the shaded or lower side), apical dominance, root formation on cuttings, fruit development; synthetic auxins (2,4-D) are herbicides |
| **Gibberellins** | Stem elongation (bolting), seed germination (trigger amylase in cereal seeds), larger seedless grapes |
| **Cytokinins** | Cell division, delay aging, promote side buds (work against auxin) |
| **Abscisic acid (ABA)** | **Stress hormone**: closes stomata in drought, keeps seeds dormant |
| **Ethylene** | A gas: **fruit ripening** ("one bad apple"), leaf drop (abscission), the triple response in seedlings |
| Brassinosteroids, jasmonates, salicylic acid, strigolactones | Growth; defense against insects; disease resistance; branching and mycorrhizae |

**Tropisms:** phototropism (light), gravitropism (gravity), thigmotropism (touch, tendrils). **Nastic movements** aren't directional (the *Mimosa* folds its leaves when touched, flowers open and close).

**Photoperiodism:** plants measure the length of the **night** with **phytochrome**, which switches between Pr (absorbs red, 660 nm) and Pfr (absorbs far-red, 730 nm). **Short-day plants** (really long-night: poinsettia, chrysanthemum) flower when nights are longer than a critical length. **Long-day plants** (spinach, iris) flower when nights are shorter. **Day-neutral plants** (tomato) don't care. A flash of red light in the middle of the night "breaks" a long night.

**Other responses:** vernalization (needing cold to flower), seed dormancy and germination (imbibition, enzymes digest the endosperm), and **circadian rhythms**.

## Mineral nutrition

| Nutrient | Role | Deficiency sign |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen (N)** | Proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll | Yellowing (**chlorosis**) of **older leaves** first (mobile) |
| **Phosphorus (P)** | ATP, nucleic acids, membranes | Purple or dark leaves, stunted growth |
| **Potassium (K)** | Stomata, enzyme activation, osmotic balance | Scorched leaf edges on older leaves |
| Calcium (Ca) | Cell walls, membranes, signaling | Damage to young tissue (blossom-end rot in tomatoes) |
| Magnesium (Mg) | **Center of chlorophyll** | Chlorosis **between the veins** of older leaves |
| Sulfur (S) | Amino acids (cysteine, methionine) | General yellowing of **young leaves** |
| Iron (Fe) | Chlorophyll synthesis, electron carriers | Chlorosis between veins of **young** leaves (immobile) |

- **Macronutrients:** C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S. **Micronutrients:** Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni.
- **Mobile nutrients** (N, P, K, Mg) move from old leaves to new ones, so **older leaves show deficiency first**. Immobile ones (Ca, Fe, S, B) show up in **young leaves** first.
- Fertilizer labels show **N-P-K** percentages.

## Reproduction and life cycles

### Alternation of generations

![Alternation of generations](img:bot-alternation)

Every plant alternates between:

- A **diploid sporophyte** (2n) that makes spores by **meiosis**.
- A **haploid gametophyte** (n) that makes gametes by **mitosis**.

| Group | Dominant generation | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Bryophytes** (mosses, liverworts, hornworts) | **Gametophyte** (the green plant you see); the sporophyte is a stalk with a capsule that depends on it | Need water for sperm to swim; no true vascular tissue; rhizoids instead of roots |
| **Ferns and allies** (seedless vascular) | **Sporophyte** (the leafy fern); a tiny, free-living, heart-shaped gametophyte (prothallus) | Sori (spore clusters) under fronds; fiddleheads; still need water for fertilization |
| **Gymnosperms** | Sporophyte; gametophytes are microscopic and inside cones | **Seeds and pollen**, no water needed; male cones make pollen, female cones carry ovules |
| **Angiosperms** | Sporophyte; male gametophyte = pollen grain (3 cells), female = embryo sac (7 cells, 8 nuclei) | **Flowers and fruits**, **double fertilization** |

### Flowers

![Labeled parts of a flower](img:bot-flower)

- **Sepals** (calyx), **petals** (corolla), **stamens** (anther + filament: male, the androecium), and **carpels/pistils** (stigma + style + ovary: female, the gynoecium).
- **Complete** flowers have all four parts; **perfect** flowers have both stamens and carpels. **Monoecious** plants have separate male and female flowers on one plant (corn); **dioecious** species have them on separate plants (holly, ginkgo).
- **Ovary position:** superior (above where petals attach) vs inferior (below).
- **Pollination:** wind (small flowers, lots of pollen, feathery stigmas: grasses, oaks) vs animal (bright colors, scent, nectar). Bees see UV patterns; hummingbirds like red tubular flowers; moths like white, night-scented flowers; bats like large, pale, musky ones.
- **Double fertilization:** one sperm fertilizes the egg (**zygote, 2n**) and the other fuses with two polar nuclei (**endosperm, 3n**, food for the embryo).

### Seeds and fruits

- **Seed:** embryo (radicle, hypocotyl, epicotyl, plumule, cotyledons) + food (endosperm or cotyledons) + seed coat. **Monocots** have one cotyledon; corn's endosperm is the food. **Dicots** have two; beans store food in their cotyledons.
- **Germination:** epigeal (cotyledons pushed above ground: beans) vs hypogeal (stay underground: peas, corn).
- **Fruit** = a mature ovary. **Simple** fruits (one ovary): fleshy (berry: tomato, grape; drupe: peach, cherry; pome: apple, an accessory fruit) or dry (legume, capsule, achene, nut, grain/caryopsis, samara). **Aggregate** fruits (many ovaries in one flower: raspberry, strawberry). **Multiple** fruits (many flowers fused: pineapple, fig).
- **Dispersal:** wind (dandelion, maple samaras), water (coconut), animals (eaten and passed, or hooked burrs), explosive (touch-me-not).

### Asexual (vegetative) reproduction

Runners, rhizomes, tubers, bulbs, apomixis (seeds without fertilization: dandelions). In horticulture: cuttings, grafting (scion on rootstock), layering, division and **tissue culture** (micropropagation).

## Plant diversity and classification

![Phylogeny of land plants](img:bot-phylogeny)

| Divide | Groups |
|---|---|
| Algae vs land plants (**embryophytes**) | Green algae (charophytes, the closest relatives of land plants), red and brown algae (not plants in the strict sense). Land plants protect the embryo in the parent. |
| **Cryptogams** vs seed plants | Cryptogams reproduce by spores (algae, bryophytes, ferns, lycophytes); "hidden" reproduction |
| Non-vascular vs vascular | Bryophytes vs **tracheophytes** (lycophytes, ferns, seed plants) |
| Gymnosperms vs angiosperms | "Naked seeds" (conifers, cycads, ginkgo, gnetophytes) vs seeds in fruits |
| **Monocots vs eudicots** | See table below |
| Woody vs herbaceous | With or without secondary growth (wood) |

| Feature | Monocots | Eudicots |
|---|---|---|
| Cotyledons | 1 | 2 |
| Leaf veins | Parallel | Net |
| Flower parts | Multiples of **3** | Multiples of **4 or 5** |
| Stem bundles | Scattered | Ring |
| Roots | Fibrous | Taproot |
| Pollen | One opening | Three openings |
| Secondary growth | Usually none | Often |
| Examples | Grasses, lilies, orchids, palms | Roses, beans, oaks, sunflowers |

**Important families to recognize:** Poaceae (grasses: corn, wheat, rice), Fabaceae (legumes: pea flowers, pods, nodules), Asteraceae (composites: daisy "flower" heads made of many florets), Rosaceae (roses, apples, strawberries; 5 petals), Brassicaceae (mustard and cabbage; 4 petals in a cross), Solanaceae (nightshades: tomato, potato, pepper), Lamiaceae (mints: square stems, opposite leaves), Orchidaceae (orchids, the largest family), Pinaceae (pines, spruce, fir).

**Lycophytes and horsetails:** club mosses (*Lycopodium*), spikemosses (*Selaginella*, which can be heterosporous), quillworts, and horsetails (*Equisetum*, with silica-rich stems; grouped with ferns today).

## Evolution and paleobotany

| Time | Event |
|---|---|
| ~1 billion+ years ago | Green algae |
| ~470 million years ago (Ordovician) | First land plants (spores like bryophytes'); cuticle, stomata later |
| ~425 million years ago (Silurian) | First vascular plants (*Cooksonia*) |
| ~400 million years ago (Devonian) | *Rhynie chert* fossils; roots and leaves evolve; first trees (*Archaeopteris*); first seeds (~360 million years ago) |
| ~359–299 million years ago (Carboniferous) | Coal swamp forests of giant lycophytes (*Lepidodendron*, *Sigillaria*), horsetails (*Calamites*) and seed ferns; formed most coal |
| Permian–Mesozoic | Gymnosperms dominate (conifers, cycads, ginkgos) |
| ~130 million years ago (Cretaceous) | Angiosperms appear and radiate (*Archaefructus*; *Amborella* is the oldest living lineage) |
| ~30–8 million years ago | Grasslands spread, C₄ grasses rise |

**Key innovations:** cuticle and stomata (stop drying out), vascular tissue (grow tall), lignin, roots, leaves (microphylls in lycophytes vs megaphylls), seeds, pollen, flowers and fruit. **Fossil types:** compressions, impressions, petrifactions (permineralized wood), coal balls, pollen (palynology, used to reconstruct past climates).

## Ecology, cycles and competition

- **Energy:** plants are producers. About 1% of sunlight is captured, and roughly 10% passes to each higher trophic level. Global photosynthesis fixes on the order of 100 billion tonnes of carbon per year (land and ocean about equally).
- **Carbon cycle:** photosynthesis takes CO₂ out; respiration, decay and burning put it back; forests, soils and peat are huge carbon stores. **Nitrogen cycle:** fixation (bacteria in nodules, lightning, the Haber process), nitrification, assimilation, ammonification, denitrification. **Water cycle:** transpiration moves most of the water that goes from land to the air.
- **Competition:** for light (tall growth, shade tolerance), water and nutrients (root spread), and pollinators. **Allelopathy:** chemicals that harm neighbors (black walnut's juglone). **Succession:** pioneer species (lichens, mosses) → grasses and shrubs → climax forest; primary (bare rock) vs secondary (after fire or clearing). **Invasive species** (kudzu, purple loosestrife) outcompete natives.
- **Other relationships:** parasites (dodder, mistletoe), epiphytes (orchids, Spanish moss), and mutualism (mycorrhizae, nodules, pollinators).

## Applied botany

### Genetics, reproduction and GMOs

- **Mendel used peas.** Know monohybrid and dihybrid crosses (3:1, 9:3:3:1), incomplete dominance (snapdragons: red × white = pink), and polyploidy (common in plants: wheat is hexaploid, many strawberries are octoploid; it can form a new species in one generation).
- **GMOs:** genes inserted using *Agrobacterium tumefaciens* (its Ti plasmid) or a gene gun, and now **CRISPR** editing. Examples: **Bt crops** (a *Bacillus thuringiensis* toxin gene kills caterpillars), **herbicide-tolerant** crops (Roundup Ready, glyphosate), **Golden Rice** (β-carotene for vitamin A), virus-resistant **papaya** (saved Hawaii's papaya industry from ringspot virus), and the non-browning Arctic apple. Debates: gene flow to wild relatives, resistant pests and weeds, labeling, corporate control vs higher yields and less pesticide.

### Food, products, horticulture and aquaculture

- **Staple crops:** maize, rice and wheat provide most of the world's calories. **The Green Revolution** (Norman Borlaug, 1960s): dwarf high-yield wheat and rice, fertilizers and irrigation.
- **Crop origins:** Fertile Crescent (wheat, barley, lentils), Mesoamerica (maize, beans, squash), Andes (potato, tomato), China (rice, soybeans).
- **Plant products:** fiber (cotton, flax, hemp), wood and paper, rubber (*Hevea*), oils, spices, coffee, tea, cacao, biofuels (ethanol from corn and sugarcane).
- **Horticulture:** soil (texture, pH, organic matter), irrigation, pruning, propagation, crop rotation, integrated pest management, greenhouses. **Hydroponics** (roots in a nutrient solution) and **aquaponics** (fish waste feeds plants, plants clean the water for the fish). **Aquaculture** of plants includes seaweed farming (kelp, nori) and rice paddies.

### Plant diseases

| Cause | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Fungi** (most plant diseases) | Wheat stem rust, powdery mildew, Dutch elm disease, chestnut blight (wiped out the American chestnut), corn smut, ergot (on rye; poisoning linked to "St. Anthony's fire") |
| **Oomycetes** ("water molds") | *Phytophthora infestans*, the potato late blight behind the **Irish Potato Famine** (1845–49); sudden oak death |
| **Bacteria** | Fire blight (apples, pears), crown gall (*Agrobacterium*), citrus greening (HLB) |
| **Viruses** | **Tobacco mosaic virus** (the first virus discovered), papaya ringspot |
| Nematodes, parasitic plants | Root-knot nematodes, dodder, witchweed (*Striga*) |
| Nutrient deficiencies | See the nutrient table |

The **disease triangle**: a disease needs a susceptible host, a pathogen and a favorable environment. Plant defenses include the cuticle and bark, the hypersensitive response (killing infected cells), systemic acquired resistance (salicylic acid) and chemicals like alkaloids and tannins.

### Biochemistry

Carbohydrates (cellulose, starch, sucrose, pectin), lignin, proteins and enzymes (RuBisCO, the most abundant protein on Earth), lipids (cutin, suberin, waxes, oils) and **secondary metabolites**:

- **Alkaloids:** caffeine, nicotine, morphine, quinine.
- **Terpenes:** menthol, rubber, taxol.
- **Phenolics:** tannins, lignin, flavonoids, anthocyanins.

| Drug | Plant | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Aspirin (salicylic acid) | Willow bark (*Salix*), meadowsweet | Pain, fever |
| Morphine, codeine | Opium poppy | Pain |
| Quinine | Cinchona bark | Malaria |
| **Artemisinin** | Sweet wormwood (*Artemisia annua*) | Malaria (Nobel 2015, Tu Youyou) |
| Digoxin | Foxglove (*Digitalis*) | Heart failure |
| **Taxol (paclitaxel)** | Pacific yew | Cancer |

**Environmental uses:** **phytoremediation** (sunflowers took up radioactive cesium and strontium near Chernobyl; plants that hyperaccumulate metals), erosion control, windbreaks, constructed wetlands treating wastewater, urban trees cooling cities, reforestation and carbon storage.

### Why plant diversity matters

Food security (crop wild relatives carry resistance genes), medicines (many drugs come from plants, and most species haven't been studied), ecosystem services (pollination, water, soil), and resilience to disease (the Irish famine relied on a single potato variety). Conservation tools include **seed banks** (Svalbard Global Seed Vault), botanical gardens and protected habitats.

## How to study

1. **Weeks 1–2:** cells, tissues, roots, stems, leaves. Practice with **real slides** or online cross-section images until you can tell monocot from dicot at a glance.
2. **Weeks 3–4:** photosynthesis (C₃/C₄/CAM), transport, hormones and nutrition.
3. **Weeks 5–6:** life cycles, flowers, fruits, diversity and evolution.
4. **Weeks 7–8:** the applied topics: GMOs, diseases, medicine, food, history.
5. **Always:** timed practice tests; dissect flowers from the grocery store; take a phone ID app on walks.

**The notes sheet:** monocot vs dicot table, the hormone table, C₃/C₄/CAM, nutrient deficiencies, the life-cycle table, the plant drugs table, a history timeline and key family features.

## Common pitfalls

- Thinking the O₂ from photosynthesis comes from CO₂. It comes from **water** (PSII).
- Mixing up xylem (water, dead cells) and phloem (sugar, living cells).
- Calling a potato a root (it's a **stem** tuber) or a sweet potato a stem (it's a **root**).
- Forgetting **night length** is what photoperiodic plants actually measure.
- Calling the moss you see the sporophyte. In mosses, the green plant is the **gametophyte**.

<details><summary>2. Why are C₄ plants better than C₃ plants in hot, dry conditions?</summary>

A diploid (2n) zygote and triploid (3n) endosperm.

Ethylene ripens fruit; abscisic acid (ABA) closes stomata.
