## The hydrologic cycle and water budgets

![The water cycle](img:dp-water-cycle)

### Processes (fluxes)

| Process | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Evaporation** | Liquid to vapor from oceans, lakes, soil |
| **Transpiration** | Water vapor lost through plant stomata |
| **Evapotranspiration (ET)** | Evaporation + transpiration together; **potential ET** is how much would happen with unlimited water |
| **Sublimation** | Ice directly to vapor (snowfields in dry, windy, sunny places) |
| **Condensation** | Vapor to liquid droplets (clouds) when air cools to its dew point |
| **Precipitation** | Rain, snow, sleet, hail falling to the surface |
| **Interception** | Precipitation caught by vegetation before reaching the ground |
| **Infiltration** | Water entering the soil surface |
| **Percolation** | Water moving down through soil and rock to the water table (recharge) |
| **Runoff (overland flow)** | Water flowing over the surface when rain exceeds infiltration (**Hortonian flow**) or the ground is saturated (**saturation excess flow**) |
| **Throughflow (interflow)** | Water moving laterally through soil to streams |
| **Baseflow** | Groundwater feeding streams between storms |

### Reservoirs and residence time

| Reservoir | Share of Earth's water | Typical residence time |
|---|---|---|
| Oceans | ~96.5% | ~3,000 years |
| Ice caps and glaciers | ~1.7% (~69% of **fresh** water) | Hundreds to hundreds of thousands of years |
| Groundwater | ~1.7% (~30% of fresh water) | Weeks (shallow) to 10,000+ years (deep) |
| Lakes | ~0.013% | Years to centuries |
| Soil moisture | ~0.001% | Weeks to months |
| Atmosphere | ~0.001% | **~9 days** |
| Rivers | ~0.0002% | Weeks |
| Living things | ~0.0001% | About a week |

Only ~2.5% of Earth's water is fresh, and **less than 1%** of all water is easily accessible fresh water.

**Residence time = volume of the reservoir / flux in (or out).** Example: a lake of 5 × 10⁸ m³ with an outflow of 2 × 10⁷ m³/yr has a residence time of 25 years.

### Water budgets

**P = Q + ET ± ΔS**

Precipitation = runoff (streamflow) + evapotranspiration ± change in storage (soil, groundwater, snow, lakes). For a basin over many years, ΔS ≈ 0.

> [!TIP]
> Example: a 200 km² watershed gets 900 mm of rain a year and its river carries away 300 mm. ET ≈ 900 − 300 = 600 mm (assuming no storage change). Runoff ratio = 300/900 = 0.33.

A **Thornthwaite water budget** compares monthly precipitation with potential ET: a surplus fills soil moisture, then becomes runoff; a deficit draws soil moisture down.

### The critical zone

The **critical zone** is Earth's thin outer skin from the treetops down to the bottom of groundwater, where rock, soil, water, air and life interact. It controls water quality, soil formation and the partitioning of rain into runoff, ET and recharge. NSF funds **Critical Zone Observatories** (now the Critical Zone Collaborative Network) to study it.

## Climatic controls on fresh water

- **Precipitation patterns:** the most rain falls in the **ITCZ** near the equator (rising air) and in the mid-latitude storm tracks. Deserts sit near **30° N and S** under the subtropical highs (sinking air).
- **Seasonality:** monsoons (Southern Asia), Mediterranean climates (wet winters, dry summers), snowmelt-dominated regimes (the western U.S. gets most of its streamflow from spring snowmelt).
- **Intensity:** short intense storms produce more runoff than the same rain spread out.

**Topographic and geographic controls:**

![Orographic precipitation and the rain shadow](img:dp-rain-shadow)

- **Orographic lift:** air forced up a mountain cools adiabatically, condenses and drops rain on the **windward** side. On the **leeward** side, air descends and warms, making a dry **rain shadow** (the Sierra Nevada and Nevada's Great Basin; the Cascades and eastern Washington; the Andes and the Atacama, partly).
- **Continentality:** interiors far from oceans have bigger temperature swings and often less moisture.
- **Lake-effect snow:** cold air passing over warmer, unfrozen lakes picks up moisture and dumps heavy snow on the downwind shore (east and south of the Great Lakes: Buffalo, Tug Hill).
- **Elevation:** temperature drops about 6.5 °C per 1,000 m; more precipitation falls as snow at higher elevations.

**The arid–semiarid–humid spectrum:**

| | Humid | Semiarid | Arid |
|---|---|---|---|
| P vs ET | P > potential ET | P somewhat < potential ET | P ≪ potential ET |
| Streams | **Perennial**, gaining (fed by groundwater) | Intermittent | **Ephemeral** (flow only after rain), often **losing** (lose water to the ground) |
| Landforms | Rounded hills, dense vegetation, thick soils | Grasslands, gullies | **Playas** (dry lake beds), **alluvial fans**, bajadas, arroyos and wadis, **evaporites** (halite, gypsum), salt flats |
| Drainage | Usually exterior (to the ocean) | | Often **endorheic** (interior basins that end in lakes or playas: the Great Basin, the Dead Sea) |

## Drainage basins and stream networks

![A drainage basin](img:dp-watershed)

- **Watershed (drainage basin, catchment):** all the land that drains to a point on a stream. **Drainage divides** separate watersheds (the **Continental Divide**).
- **Basin characteristics:** area, shape (elongated vs circular, affecting flood peaks), relief, slope, soils, geology, land cover.
- **Stream order (Strahler):** first-order streams have no tributaries. Two first-order streams joining make a second-order stream, and so on. A higher-order stream joining a lower-order one **doesn't** change the order. The Mississippi is about 10th order. **Bifurcation ratio** = number of streams of order n / number of order n + 1 (typically 3–5).
- **Drainage density** = total stream length / basin area (km/km²). High density: impermeable rock or clay, sparse vegetation, arid or semiarid climates with intense rain. Low density: permeable sand, limestone, dense forest. **Drainage texture** is the spacing of streams: fine (high density) vs coarse (low density).

**Drainage patterns:**

![Common drainage patterns](img:dp-drainage-patterns)

| Pattern | Looks like | Geology |
|---|---|---|
| **Dendritic** | Tree branches | Uniform rock, no structural control (most common) |
| **Trellis** | Long parallel streams with short right-angle tributaries | **Folded** sedimentary rocks: Appalachian Valley and Ridge |
| **Rectangular** | Right-angle bends | **Jointed** or faulted bedrock |
| **Radial** | Spokes out from a center | A **volcano** or dome |
| **Centripetal** | Streams flowing inward | A basin or crater |
| **Annular** | Rings | An eroded dome with alternating hard and soft layers |
| **Parallel** | Parallel streams | Steep uniform slopes |
| **Deranged** | Chaotic, with many lakes and swamps | Recently **glaciated** land (northern Minnesota, Canadian Shield) |

## Fluvial sediment processes and channel form

### Channel types

![Braided, meandering and straight channels](img:dp-channel-types)

| Type | Description | Conditions |
|---|---|---|
| **Straight** | Rare for long; often structurally controlled | Sinuosity < ~1.05 |
| **Meandering** | Single channel with loops | Gentle slope, fine sediment, cohesive banks, steady flow; sinuosity > **1.5** |
| **Braided** | Many channels separated by gravel bars | **Abundant coarse sediment**, steep slopes, variable flow (glacial outwash, arid regions) |
| **Anastomosing** | Multiple stable channels separated by vegetated islands | Low gradient, cohesive banks, often aggrading |

**Sinuosity = channel length / straight-line (valley) distance.** Example: 18 km of channel between two points 12 km apart → sinuosity 1.5.

### Sediment

- **Grain size (Wentworth scale):** boulder (> 256 mm), cobble (64–256), pebble (4–64), granule (2–4), **sand** (1/16–2 mm), **silt** (1/256–1/16), **clay** (< 1/256 mm). The φ scale: φ = −log₂(d in mm).
- **Sorting:** well-sorted (one size: wind or beach sand) vs poorly sorted (mixed: glacial till, debris flows). **Rounding:** angular grains have traveled less; rounded grains have traveled far. **Sphericity** is how close a grain is to a sphere.
- **Hjulström curve:** shows the velocity needed to **erode**, **transport** or **deposit** grains of each size. Sand (~0.2–0.5 mm) is **easiest to erode**. Clay and silt need **higher** velocities to erode than sand because of cohesion, but stay suspended at very low velocities once moving.

![The Hjulström curve](img:dp-hjulstrom)

### Loads, competence and capacity

| Load | How it moves | Share |
|---|---|---|
| **Dissolved load** | Ions in solution (from chemical weathering) | High in limestone and arid areas |
| **Suspended load** | Clay and silt carried in the water column (makes water muddy) | Usually the **largest** part |
| **Bed load** | Sand and gravel rolling, sliding (traction) and bouncing (**saltation**) along the bottom | Small but shapes channels |
| Wash load | The finest suspended material, which rarely settles | |

- **Competence:** the **largest grain** a stream can move (rises with velocity, roughly with v² to v⁶ depending on the measure).
- **Capacity:** the **total amount** of sediment it can carry (rises with discharge).
- **Sediment supply** is controlled by climate, vegetation, relief, rock type, land use and dams.

### Bedforms

As flow strengthens (rising **Froude number**, Fr = v / √(gd)):

| Regime | Bedforms | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Lower flow regime (Fr < 1, **subcritical**) | **Ripples** (cm scale) → **dunes** (larger) | Migrate downstream; build **cross-bedding** |
| Transition | Washed-out dunes, plane bed | |
| Upper flow regime (Fr ≈ 1 or more, **supercritical**) | **Plane bed** → **antidunes** | Antidunes migrate **upstream** and are in phase with surface waves |

**Bars:** point bars (inside of meanders), mid-channel bars (braided rivers), alternate bars and longitudinal bars.

## Fluvial geomorphology

### Longitudinal profile and base level

![A stream's longitudinal profile](img:dp-long-profile)

- A typical river is **concave-up**: steep near the head, gentle near the mouth. **Gradient = drop in elevation / distance** (m/km).
- **Base level:** the lowest level a stream can erode to. **Ultimate base level** = sea level. **Local (temporary) base levels:** lakes, dams, resistant rock layers.
- **Lowering base level** (sea level drops, land uplifts) → **downcutting**, incised meanders, **stream terraces** (former floodplains left above the river). **Raising base level** (a dam) → deposition upstream (sediment fills the reservoir) and erosion downstream ("hungry water").

### Graded streams and dynamic equilibrium

A **graded stream** has adjusted its slope, channel shape and velocity so it carries exactly the sediment supplied, with no net erosion or deposition. It's in **dynamic equilibrium**: change one variable (discharge, sediment load, base level) and the stream adjusts. **Lane's balance**: Q_s × D₅₀ ∝ Q_w × S (sediment load × grain size ∝ water discharge × slope).

### Erosional features and processes

- **Upper course** (steep): **V-shaped valleys**, **waterfalls**, **rapids**, potholes, gorges. **Knickpoints** are sharp breaks in slope (often at resistant rock or a fault) that migrate upstream by **headward erosion** (Niagara Falls retreated ~11 km in ~12,000 years).
- **Processes:**
  - **Hydraulic action:** water's force alone, lifting and plucking.
  - **Abrasion (corrasion):** sediment grinding the bed and banks (makes potholes).
  - **Attrition:** particles bumping each other, getting smaller and rounder.
  - **Solution (corrosion):** dissolving rock (limestone).
  - **Downcutting** (vertical erosion) dominates upstream; **lateral erosion** (meandering) dominates downstream.
- **Stream capture (piracy):** a stream eroding headward breaks into another basin and steals its flow, leaving a **wind gap** (dry valley) and an **elbow of capture**.

### Channel and floodplain landforms

![Meander features: cut bank, point bar, oxbow lake](img:dp-meander)

- **Meanders:** flow is fastest on the **outside** of a bend, eroding a steep **cut bank**. It's slowest on the **inside**, depositing a **point bar**. Helical flow moves sediment across. Meanders migrate downstream and widen the valley.
- **Oxbow lakes:** a meander neck is cut off (usually in a flood), leaving a crescent-shaped lake. It fills with sediment to form a **meander scar**.
- **Floodplain:** flat land built by lateral migration (point bars) and overbank flooding.
- **Natural levees:** ridges along the banks where floods drop coarser sediment first. Behind them lie **backswamps** and yazoo streams (tributaries running parallel because they can't cross the levee).
- **Deltas** (at a standing body of water): topset, foreset and bottomset beds. Shapes: **bird's-foot** (river-dominated: Mississippi), **arcuate/fan** (wave-influenced: Nile), **cuspate** (wave-dominated: Tiber), and **tide-dominated** (Ganges–Brahmaputra). Delta lobes switch by **avulsion**.
- **Alluvial fans:** cone-shaped deposits where a steep mountain stream exits onto flat land (common in arid regions: Death Valley). Coalescing fans form a **bajada**.

## Streamflow and floods

### Flow regimes

| Regime | Flows | Fed by |
|---|---|---|
| **Perennial** | Year-round | Baseflow from groundwater (channel below the water table: a **gaining stream**) |
| **Intermittent** | Seasonally | The water table rises above the channel in wet seasons |
| **Ephemeral** | Only during and right after rain | Runoff only; channel always above the water table (a **losing stream**) |

### Discharge and open-channel flow

- **Discharge Q = A × v** (cross-sectional area × mean velocity), in m³/s (cms) or ft³/s (cfs). For a rectangular channel, A = width × mean depth.
- Downstream, discharge usually increases (tributaries), and so do width, depth and often mean velocity (despite a gentler slope, because the channel is more efficient).
- **Hydraulic radius** R = A / wetted perimeter P. A larger R means less friction (a more efficient channel).
- **Manning's equation:** **v = (1/n) R^(2/3) S^(1/2)** (SI units), where n is the roughness coefficient (~0.03 for a clean natural channel, higher with boulders and vegetation) and S is the slope. In U.S. units, there's a 1.49 factor: v = (1.49/n) R^(2/3) S^(1/2).
- **Chézy equation:** v = C √(RS).
- **Froude number** Fr = v / √(gD): Fr < 1 subcritical (tranquil), Fr > 1 supercritical (rapid, shooting). A hydraulic jump is where supercritical flow suddenly becomes subcritical.
- **Reynolds number** separates laminar from turbulent flow; almost all rivers are turbulent.

> [!TIP]
> Example: a channel 20 m wide and 2 m deep (rectangular) with slope 0.001 and n = 0.035. A = 40 m², P = 20 + 2 + 2 = 24 m, R = 1.67 m. v = (1/0.035)(1.67)^(2/3)(0.001)^(1/2) = 28.6 × 1.41 × 0.0316 ≈ 1.27 m/s. Q = 40 × 1.27 ≈ 51 m³/s.

### Hydrographs

![A storm hydrograph](img:dp-hydrograph)

A **storm hydrograph** plots discharge vs time after a rain event:

- **Rising limb** → **peak discharge** → **falling (recession) limb**.
- **Lag time:** from peak rainfall to peak discharge.
- **Baseflow** (groundwater contribution, under the curve) vs **stormflow (quickflow)** (runoff).
- **Flashy hydrographs** (short lag, high peak) come from small, steep, urbanized, impermeable, saturated or circular basins. **Subdued** ones (long lag, low peak) come from large, gentle, forested, permeable basins.

### Floods and recurrence intervals

- **Recurrence (return) interval:** **T = (n + 1) / m**, where n = number of years of record and m = rank of the flood (1 = largest).
- **Annual exceedance probability:** **P = 1/T**. A "100-year flood" has a **1% chance** every year. It can happen two years in a row.
- **Probability of at least one T-year flood in N years:** **1 − (1 − 1/T)ᴺ**. The chance of a 100-year flood during a 30-year mortgage is 1 − 0.99³⁰ ≈ **26%**.
- **Flood frequency curves:** plot discharge vs recurrence interval on log-probability paper and read off the 10-, 50- and 100-year floods.
- **Flood types:** riverine (snowmelt, prolonged rain), **flash floods** (intense rain, dam or ice-jam failure), coastal (storm surge), urban. Notable: Mississippi 1927 and 1993; Johnstown 1889 (dam failure); Big Thompson 1976 (flash flood); Hurricane Harvey 2017.

> [!TIP]
> Example: in 49 years of record, the 2nd-largest flood has T = (49 + 1)/2 = 25 years, P = 4%.

## Groundwater and karst

### Subsurface zones

![Groundwater zones and aquifers](img:dp-aquifer)

- **Zone of aeration (vadose zone, unsaturated):** pores contain both air and water. Includes soil moisture and the **capillary fringe** (just above the water table, where water is pulled up by capillary action; thicker in fine-grained material like clay than in coarse sand).
- **Water table:** the top of the saturated zone, where water pressure equals atmospheric pressure. It roughly follows the land surface but more gently.
- **Zone of saturation (phreatic):** all pores filled with water: **groundwater**.
- **Perched water table:** a small saturated zone held up by a lens of impermeable clay above the main water table.

### Aquifers and properties

- **Porosity** = void volume / total volume. Clay can have high porosity (~40–60%) but very low permeability. Well-sorted, rounded sediment has high porosity; poor sorting and cementation lower it.
- **Permeability (hydraulic conductivity, K):** how easily water flows through. Depends on pore size and **connectedness**. Gravel and sand are high; clay and unfractured granite are very low.
- **Specific yield** (water that drains by gravity) + **specific retention** (water held by surface tension) = porosity.
- **Aquifer:** permeable rock or sediment that yields useful water (sand, gravel, sandstone, fractured or karst limestone). **Aquitard / aquiclude:** low-permeability layers (clay, shale).
- **Unconfined aquifer:** open to recharge from above; its top is the water table.
- **Confined aquifer:** sandwiched between aquitards; water is under pressure. The **potentiometric surface** is the level water would rise to in a well.
- **Artesian well:** in a confined aquifer, water rises above the aquifer's top. **Flowing artesian well:** the potentiometric surface is above the ground, so water flows out on its own. Examples: the **Great Artesian Basin** (Australia), the Dakota Sandstone.
- **Major U.S. aquifers:** **Ogallala (High Plains)** (huge, heavily pumped for irrigation, declining), Floridan (karst limestone), Edwards (Texas karst).

### Groundwater flow

- **Hydraulic head** h = elevation head + pressure head (measured as the water level in a well, relative to a datum). Water flows from **high head to low head**, not necessarily downhill.
- **Hydraulic gradient** i = Δh / L (change in head / distance).
- **Darcy's law:** **Q = K A (Δh / L)**, or specific discharge q = Q/A = Ki.
- **Average linear (seepage) velocity:** **v = K i / n_e** (n_e = effective porosity), which is how fast water and contaminants actually move.

> [!TIP]
> Example: two wells 500 m apart have heads of 120 m and 115 m. i = 5/500 = 0.01. In sand with K = 10 m/day and n_e = 0.25, v = 10 × 0.01 / 0.25 = 0.4 m/day. A contaminant takes about 1,250 days (~3.4 years) to travel 500 m.

### Recharge and discharge

- **Recharge areas:** where water enters an aquifer (infiltration through permeable soil, losing streams, outcrops of confined aquifers).
- **Discharge areas:** where groundwater exits (springs, gaining streams, wetlands, wells, seeps).
- **Cone of depression:** pumping a well lowers the water table in a cone around it (**drawdown**). Overlapping cones from neighboring wells can make shallow wells go dry or reverse flow toward pollution.

### Surface water–groundwater interaction

- **Gaining stream:** water table above the stream; groundwater flows **into** it (humid areas).
- **Losing stream:** water table below; stream water flows **out** into the ground (arid areas; can be disconnected from the water table).
- **Hyporheic zone:** the mixing zone under and beside the stream bed.
- **Saltwater intrusion:** fresh water floats on denser salt water in coastal aquifers. The **Ghyben–Herzberg relation**: for every **1 m** of fresh water above sea level, the fresh–salt interface lies about **40 m** below sea level (z ≈ 40h). Overpumping lowers h, so salt water rises (40 times as much!) and contaminates wells (Florida, the Netherlands, many islands).

### Karst

![A karst landscape with sinkholes and caves](img:dp-karst)

**Karst** forms where **soluble rock** (limestone, dolomite, gypsum, salt) dissolves. Rain absorbs CO₂ (from the air and soil) to form **carbonic acid**: CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃, which dissolves calcite: **CaCO₃ + H₂CO₃ → Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻**.

| Feature | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Sinkholes (dolines)** | Depressions from dissolution or collapse. **Solution** sinkholes form slowly; **cover-collapse** sinkholes form suddenly when the soil roof over a cavity fails (Florida). |
| **Uvalas** | Merged sinkholes |
| **Solution valleys, poljes** | Large flat-floored karst depressions |
| **Caves** | Dissolved at or just below the water table; enlarged along joints and bedding planes. Mammoth Cave (KY) is the world's longest known cave system. |
| **Speleothems** | Cave deposits from dripping water losing CO₂: **stalactites** (hang from the ceiling, "c" for ceiling), **stalagmites** (grow up from the floor), columns, flowstone, soda straws |
| **Disappearing (sinking) streams** | Streams that vanish into a **swallet (swallow hole)** and flow underground |
| **Springs (resurgences)** | Where underground water reemerges (Florida's first-magnitude springs, > 2.8 m³/s) |
| **Karst towers, cockpit karst** | Tropical karst landscapes (Guilin, China; Puerto Rico) |
| **Karren, limestone pavement** | Grooves and blocks (clints and grikes) on bare limestone |

**Karst hydrology:** water moves fast through conduits (km per day), so **contaminants spread quickly with little filtering**. Springs respond quickly to rain. Groundwater divides don't match surface divides, so **dye tracing** is used to map flow. About 25% of the world's people rely on karst water.

## Lakes and wetlands

### Lake origins

| Origin | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Tectonic / rift** | Lake Baikal (deepest, ~1,642 m, oldest, ~25 million years, ~20% of unfrozen fresh water), Lake Tanganyika, the Dead Sea |
| **Volcanic** | **Crater Lake** (Oregon, a caldera, deepest U.S. lake ~594 m), maar lakes, lava-dammed lakes |
| **Glacial** | Great Lakes (scoured basins), **kettle lakes** (buried ice blocks melted), **tarns/cirque lakes**, finger lakes (New York's Finger Lakes), moraine-dammed lakes |
| **Fluvial** | **Oxbow lakes**, floodplain lakes, lakes dammed by tributary sediment |
| Landslide, coastal, solution (karst), human-made | Quake Lake (MT), lagoons, sinkhole lakes, reservoirs |

### Lake water budgets

ΔStorage = (precipitation + surface inflow + groundwater inflow) − (evaporation + surface outflow + groundwater outflow). Closed (**endorheic**) lakes have no outflow, so they become **saline** (Great Salt Lake, Dead Sea, Aral Sea). Their levels rise and fall sharply with climate and water diversion.

### Physical and chemical properties

![Thermal stratification of a lake](img:dp-stratification)

- **Water is densest at 4 °C**, so ice floats and deep water in winter stays near 4 °C.
- **Summer stratification** (temperate lakes): warm **epilimnion** (mixed by wind) on top, the **metalimnion** with the **thermocline** (sharp temperature drop) in the middle, and cold, dense **hypolimnion** at the bottom. The hypolimnion can lose oxygen (decay uses it up).
- **Turnover:** in fall, the surface cools to 4 °C, density evens out and wind mixes the whole lake (fall turnover), bringing oxygen down and nutrients up. In winter, ice forms with **inverse stratification**. Spring turnover happens when ice melts and the surface warms to 4 °C.
- **Mixing types:** **dimictic** (mix twice a year: most temperate lakes), **monomictic** (once: warm or polar lakes), **polymictic** (often: shallow lakes), **meromictic** (never fully mix; permanent deep layer).
- **Trophic status:** **oligotrophic** (low nutrients, clear, deep, high oxygen: Lake Superior, Crater Lake), **mesotrophic**, **eutrophic** (high nutrients, algae, murky, low deep oxygen), hypereutrophic. **Secchi disk** depth measures clarity. **Cultural eutrophication** from fertilizer and sewage phosphorus (Lake Erie in the 1960s–70s and again with recent algal blooms).
- **Zones:** littoral (shallow, rooted plants), limnetic (open water with light), profundal (deep, dark), benthic (bottom). The **photic zone** gets enough light for photosynthesis.

### Shoreline processes

Lakes have **waves** (driven by wind; height depends on wind speed, duration and **fetch**), **seiches** (standing waves sloshing across a lake after wind or pressure changes; Lake Erie seiches can raise water by meters at one end), **longshore drift**, and features such as **beaches, spits, bars, barrier beaches, wave-cut cliffs and terraces**. Ancient shorelines (like Lake Bonneville's terraces around Salt Lake City) record past lake levels.

### Wetlands

| Type | Water source | Water chemistry | Vegetation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bog** | Mostly **precipitation** (ombrotrophic) | **Acidic, nutrient-poor** | **Sphagnum moss**, peat, carnivorous plants (pitcher plants, sundews), black spruce |
| **Fen** | **Groundwater** (minerotrophic) | Neutral to alkaline, richer in minerals | Sedges, grasses, wildflowers; also forms peat |
| **Marsh** | Surface water (rivers, tides) | Nutrient-rich | **Herbaceous** plants: cattails, reeds, grasses (freshwater and salt marshes) |
| **Swamp** | Surface water, flooding | Variable | **Woody** plants: trees (cypress, mangrove, red maple) and shrubs |

**Wetland functions:** flood storage, water filtering (removing nutrients and sediment), groundwater recharge, habitat (a large share of threatened species depend on them), carbon storage (peatlands hold ~30% of soil carbon). The U.S. has lost more than half its original wetlands. **Hydric soils** (gray with mottles, low oxygen) and **hydrophytic vegetation** are used to delineate them.

## Geologic and paleohydrologic record

![Ripple marks preserved in rock](img:dp-ripple-marks)

| Structure | Tells you |
|---|---|
| **Symmetrical ripple marks** | **Oscillating** flow (waves in shallow water) |
| **Asymmetrical (current) ripple marks** | **One-way** current; the steep side faces **downstream** |
| **Cross-bedding** | Migrating ripples or dunes (river or wind); the dip of the cross-beds shows the **current direction** |
| **Graded bedding** | Coarse at the bottom, fine on top: a **waning current** (turbidity currents, floods) |
| **Mud cracks** | Mud **dried out in air**, so the area was periodically exposed (floodplains, playas, tidal flats) |
| **Raindrop imprints** | Exposure to rain on soft mud |
| Flute and tool marks, imbrication | Current direction (imbricated pebbles dip **upstream**) |

Many of these also show **which way was up** (cross-bed tangents curve at the base, graded beds fine upward, mud cracks widen upward), which matters in folded rocks.

**Paleochannels:** old channels filled with sediment, often sand bodies in finer floodplain deposits; important aquifers and oil reservoirs. **Lacustrine deposits:** varves (yearly layer pairs in glacial lakes: light summer silt, dark winter clay), evaporites, oil shale (Green River Formation). **Paleolakes** like **Lake Bonneville** (Utah; drained catastrophically in the Bonneville Flood ~15,000 years ago) and **Lake Missoula** (Montana; repeated ice-dam failures created the **Channeled Scablands** of Washington) show past wetter climates.

## Human impacts and water quality

### Land use

- **Urbanization:** impervious surfaces (roads, roofs) → **more runoff, higher and faster flood peaks (shorter lag), less infiltration and baseflow**, warmer and more polluted streams ("urban stream syndrome").
- **Deforestation and agriculture:** more erosion and sediment, less ET, more runoff, fertilizer and pesticide pollution.
- **Mining:** **acid mine drainage** (pyrite + water + oxygen → sulfuric acid, dissolving heavy metals; orange "yellow boy" iron deposits).

### Engineering structures

| Structure | Intended effect | Side effects |
|---|---|---|
| **Dams and reservoirs** | Water supply, hydropower, flood control | Sediment trapped (reservoirs fill), downstream **erosion and incision** ("hungry water"), coasts and deltas shrink (Mississippi Delta, Nile Delta after the Aswan High Dam), blocked fish migration, changed temperature and flow, evaporation losses |
| **Levees** | Keep floods off the floodplain | Confine floods, raising flood heights downstream; failures can be catastrophic (New Orleans 2005); sediment no longer builds the floodplain |
| **Channelization** | Straighten and deepen for drainage and navigation | Faster flow, steeper gradient, more erosion and downstream flooding, lost habitat (Kissimmee River in Florida, now being restored) |
| **Diversions** | Move water to cities and farms | Shrinking lakes and rivers (**Aral Sea** shrank by ~90% after Soviet irrigation diversions; the **Colorado River** rarely reaches the sea) |
| **Groundwater pumping** | Irrigation and cities | **Aquifer depletion** (Ogallala), lowered water tables, dried springs and streams, **land subsidence** (California's Central Valley sank up to ~9 m; Mexico City), saltwater intrusion |

### Water pollution

- **Point sources:** a single, identifiable discharge (factory pipe, sewage treatment plant). Regulated by permits (Clean Water Act NPDES).
- **Nonpoint sources:** diffuse (farm runoff, urban stormwater, atmospheric deposition). The leading U.S. cause of impaired waters.
- **Types:** nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus → eutrophication, algal blooms, dead zones), sediment, pathogens (fecal coliform, *E. coli*), organic matter (high biochemical oxygen demand), heavy metals (lead, mercury, arsenic), organic chemicals (pesticides, PCBs, **PFAS** "forever chemicals"), salts (road salt), heat (thermal pollution), acid (acid rain, mine drainage), pharmaceuticals and microplastics.
- **Transport:** advection (moving with the water), dispersion and diffusion (spreading), sorption (sticking to sediment, especially clay and organic matter), degradation. **Plumes** spread down the hydraulic gradient. **LNAPLs** (light, like gasoline) float on the water table; **DNAPLs** (dense, like chlorinated solvents) sink to the aquifer's bottom.
- **Laws:** the **Clean Water Act (1972)** for surface waters (spurred partly by the 1969 Cuyahoga River fire) and the **Safe Drinking Water Act (1974)**. **Flint, Michigan (2014):** a change in water source without corrosion control leached lead from pipes.

## Measuring and monitoring fresh water

### Topographic maps

![Contour lines making a V upstream where they cross a stream](img:dp-topo-v)

- **Contour lines** connect points of equal elevation. The **contour interval** is the elevation difference between lines. Close spacing = steep; wide spacing = gentle.
- **Rule of V's:** where a contour crosses a stream, it bends into a **V that points upstream** (uphill). So the stream flows **opposite** to where the V points.
- **Watershed divides** run along ridges, crossing contours at right angles. **Streams flow perpendicular to contours.**
- **Gradient** from a map: elevation change / distance (using the scale).
- Blue lines: perennial streams (solid), intermittent (dashed); marsh symbols; depression contours (hachured) mark closed depressions like **sinkholes**.

### Mapping the water table

1. Plot well locations with their **water-level (head) elevations** (land elevation minus depth to water).
2. Draw **water table contours** by interpolating between wells (like contouring elevation).
3. Draw **flow lines perpendicular** to contours, from high to low head.
4. Calculate the hydraulic gradient between contours.

Three wells are enough to find the gradient and direction for a planar water table (the **three-point problem**).

### Stream gauging

- **Stage:** water surface height at a gauge. Measured by a staff gauge, float, pressure transducer or radar.
- **Discharge measurement:** divide the cross section into **subsections**, measure depth and velocity in each (a current meter or **acoustic Doppler current profiler**), compute Q = Σ (width × depth × velocity). The **velocity at 0.6 depth** (measured from the surface) approximates the mean, or average the 0.2 and 0.8 depth velocities for deeper water. A float method: surface velocity × ~0.85.
- **Rating curve (stage–discharge relationship):** discharge plotted against stage from many measurements. Once built, discharge is estimated continuously from stage alone. It shifts when the channel changes.
- **USGS** runs about 10,000+ stream gauges in the U.S. (data at waterdata.usgs.gov).

### Monitoring networks

- **Groundwater observation wells** (USGS networks) track water levels over time. **Piezometers** measure head at a specific depth.
- **Tracer studies:** fluorescent dyes (fluorescein, rhodamine) in karst; salt (conductivity); isotopes (**tritium** from 1950s–60s bomb tests dates young water; **δ¹⁸O and δ²H** show water's source and evaporation; ¹⁴C for old groundwater).
- **Remote sensing:** **GRACE and GRACE-FO** satellites measure changes in gravity from water storage (showing groundwater depletion in California, India), **SWOT** (2022) measures surface water heights, Landsat and Sentinel map lakes, floods and snow, InSAR measures subsidence.
- **Other tools:** rain gauges and radar (NEXRAD), SNOTEL snow sensors, weather stations, water quality sondes (temperature, dissolved oxygen, pH, conductivity, turbidity).

## How to study

1. **Build your binder in this order:** formulas with worked examples → each rules section a–k with a summary page → landform photo pages → maps (topo practice, stream patterns).
2. **Do the math repeatedly:** sinuosity, gradient, Q = Av, Manning, recurrence interval, Darcy, residence time, water budgets.
3. **Look at real data:** USGS stream gauges near you (hydrographs after storms), and USGS topographic maps (TopoView).
4. **Image practice:** be able to name a landform or drainage pattern from a photo or map in seconds.
5. Take full timed practice tests and add any missed facts to the binder.

## Common pitfalls

- Thinking the "100-year flood" happens once every 100 years exactly.
- V's on contour lines point **upstream**, not downstream.
- Confusing porosity with permeability (clay is porous but impermeable).
- Mixing up gaining (humid) and losing (arid) streams.
- Forgetting that groundwater flows from high **head**, not necessarily downhill.
- Reading a hydrograph's lag time from the start of rain instead of the **peak** of rain.
