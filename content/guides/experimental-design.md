## How the event runs

The supervisor gives a topic (like "the effect of ramp height on how far a marble rolls" or "factors affecting the bounce of a ball") and a bag of materials (rubber bands, paper cups, marbles, rulers, string, washers…). You must design a **controlled experiment** with one independent variable, collect real data, and write a complete report.

**Divide the work** (three people):

- **Person A:** writes Part I (problem, hypothesis, variables, materials, procedure) while the others set up.
- **Person B:** builds the setup and runs trials.
- **Person C:** records data, then starts the graph and statistics.

In Part II, split the analysis sections. Everyone proofreads at the end.

## The checklist, section by section

### Part I: Design and construction (60 points)

| Section | Points | How to get them |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Statement of the problem** | 2 | A question that names the IV and DV, **not** yes/no: "How does the height of a ramp (cm) affect the distance a marble rolls (cm)?" |
| **B. Hypothesis** | 6 | (1) Predicts a relationship between IV and DV, (2) gives a **direction** ("as height increases, distance increases"), (3) gives a **rationale** ("because higher ramps give more gravitational potential energy, which becomes kinetic energy") |
| **C. Variables** | 18 | **IV** identified and **operationally defined** (how it's measured or changed); **levels of the IV** given (at least 4–5 levels, plus the control); **DV** identified and operationally defined; **3 controlled variables** that are relevant; a **constant** |
| **D. Experimental control (standard of comparison)** | 4 | Identify it for this experiment and give a reason ("the 0 cm ramp height serves as the standard of comparison because…") |
| **E. Materials** | 4 | List **all** materials used with **quantities** ("1 marble, 2.5 cm diameter"); list **nothing extra** |
| **F. Procedure and set-up diagrams** | 13 | Numbered list, logical order, includes **repeated trials**, **multiple labeled diagrams** of the setup, detailed enough to **repeat exactly** |
| **G. Qualitative observations** | 6 | Observations about the **setup**, the **procedure**, and the **results** |
| **H. Data table** | 7 | **All raw data** with units and labels; a **condensed table** of what's graphed (e.g. means), with **one sample calculation** per derived variable; correct sig figs |

**Variables, precisely:**

- **Independent variable (IV):** what you deliberately change. Operational definition: "ramp height, measured in cm from the table to the top end of the ramp with a meter stick."
- **Dependent variable (DV):** what you measure. "Distance rolled, measured in cm from the end of the ramp to the front of the stopped marble."
- **Controlled variables:** factors you **keep the same** across trials because they could affect the DV: the same marble, ramp length, release method, floor surface.
- **Constant:** a factor that's fixed for the whole experiment and doesn't vary at all (the checklist scores one, e.g., "the same room temperature" or "the same type of ramp material"). Choose something relevant.
- **Levels of the IV:** at least 4–5 well-spaced values (e.g., 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 cm), plus the standard of comparison.

**Standard of comparison (control):** the baseline condition you compare others to. It can be a "zero" level (no added mass) or a normal condition. Justify it.

**Repeated trials:** at least **3 trials per level** (5 is better if time allows). More trials reduce random error.

### Part II: Data, analysis and conclusions (57 points Regional; 80 State/National)

| Section | Points | How to get them |
|---|---|---|
| **I. Graph** | 12 | **Appropriate type** (line or scatter graph for continuous IV; bar graph for categories), **title** ("Effect of Ramp Height on Marble Distance") and **axis labels with units**, **appropriate scale** (use most of the grid, even intervals). IV on the x-axis, DV on the y-axis. |
| **J. Statistics** | 11/14 | **Central tendency** (mean, median, mode) and **variation** (range, standard deviation, mean absolute deviation), each with **one example calculation with units** at one level of the IV, and **correct sig figs** (State/National) |
| **K. Possible experimental errors** | 6 | Two **specific** errors and how each affected the results ("the marble was released by hand, so some trials may have been pushed, making distances longer and increasing the range") |
| **L. Claim–Evidence–Reasoning** | 12/18 | Three CER blocks: **data trend**, **outliers**, and (State/National) **variation** |
| **M. Conclusion** | 8 | Restate the hypothesis; CER about whether it was supported |
| **N. Applications and recommendations** | 8/12 | **Improvements** to the experiment with rationale, **practical applications**, and **future experiments** |
| **O. Abstract** (State/National) | 10 | Problem, hypothesis, application; procedure summary and data trend; errors and improvements |

### Writing CER

Each CER block has three parts, each worth 2 points:

- **Claim:** a one-sentence answer. "As ramp height increased, distance rolled increased."
- **Evidence:** specific **numbers** from your data. "The mean distance rose from 42.3 cm at 10 cm height to 198.7 cm at 50 cm height."
- **Reasoning:** the **science** that connects evidence to claim. "A higher release point gives more gravitational potential energy (mgh), which converts to more kinetic energy at the bottom, so the marble travels farther before friction stops it."

**Outliers CER:** claim whether there were outliers (identify one with numbers, e.g. a value far outside the others or beyond 1.5 × IQR), evidence, and reasoning for its cause.

**Variation CER:** claim about how consistent trials were (which level varied most), evidence (ranges or standard deviations), reasoning (why variation was larger there).

## Statistics you need

For one level of the IV with trials 41.2, 43.8, 42.0 cm:

- **Mean** = (41.2 + 43.8 + 42.0) / 3 = **42.3 cm**
- **Median** = 42.0 cm
- **Range** = 43.8 − 41.2 = **2.6 cm**
- **Sample standard deviation:** s = √[Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)]. Deviations: −1.1, 1.5, −0.3; squares: 1.21, 2.25, 0.09; sum 3.55; / 2 = 1.775; √ = **1.33 cm**.
- **Mean absolute deviation** = (1.1 + 1.5 + 0.3) / 3 = 0.97 cm.

Show **one full example calculation, with units**, for each statistic. Apply sig fig rules (means shouldn't have more precision than your measurements, plus about one digit).

## A complete example

**Topic:** the effect of drop height on bounce height of a ball.

- **Problem:** How does the drop height (cm) of a rubber ball affect its rebound height (cm)?
- **Hypothesis:** If drop height increases, then rebound height will increase, because more gravitational potential energy is converted to kinetic energy, and the ball keeps a similar fraction of that energy after the bounce.
- **IV:** drop height, measured from the floor to the bottom of the ball with a meter stick; levels 25, 50, 75, 100 cm. **SOC:** 25 cm (smallest drop height, as a baseline).
- **DV:** rebound height, the highest point of the ball's bottom after the first bounce, read against a meter stick that a team member holds vertical beside the drop path.
- **Controlled variables:** same ball, same floor surface, released (not thrown) the same way.
- **Constant:** temperature of the room.
- **Trials:** 5 per level.
- **Graph:** scatter plot with a line of best fit (rebound vs drop height).
- **Errors:** reading the peak height by eye (parallax), releasing with slight spin.
- **Applications:** sports equipment design, safety surfaces.
- **Future:** test different ball materials, or floor surfaces.

## Practice plan

1. Learn the checklist sections by heart and make a **template** of phrases for each.
2. Do a **timed mock** every week with random household materials and a random topic. Grade it with the checklist, then fix the weakest section.
3. Practice drawing clear **labeled setup diagrams** (at least two views: before and during).
4. Practice computing standard deviation by hand and with the calculator quickly.

## Common pitfalls

- Writing a yes/no problem statement.
- Too few IV levels, or no repeated trials.
- Listing materials you didn't use (or missing quantities).
- A procedure too vague to repeat ("drop the ball and measure").
- Graphs without titles, units or proper scales; using a bar graph for continuous data.
- Generic errors ("human error") instead of specific ones with their effect.
- Not using real numbers in Evidence.
- **Making up data: ×0.25.** Always run the experiment.
