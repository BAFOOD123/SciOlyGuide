## How the event runs

You get a lab packet with tasks such as "determine the molar mass of the gas in this lighter," "measure how temperature changes the rate of this reaction" or "here's a pressure sensor; collect data as you change volume." Each task has questions on top of the lab. Some stations give you a data set with no lab at all.

Success comes from three things:

1. **Lab fluency:** reading a graduated cylinder or buret correctly, collecting a gas over water, timing reactions consistently, using sig figs.
2. **Calculation speed:** gas laws and rate laws, done fast and with units.
3. **Concept knowledge:** why rates change, what the greenhouse effect is, how ozone is destroyed.

Good teams divide tasks: one runs the lab, the other starts the calculations and questions, then you switch.

## Essential tools (all levels)

**Nomenclature and formulas.** Know the ion symbols and charges at your level:

| Level | Ions to know |
|---|---|
| Regional | Monatomic main-group ions; acetate C₂H₃O₂⁻, ammonium NH₄⁺, bicarbonate HCO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, cyanide CN⁻, hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻ |
| State & National (add) | Chromate CrO₄²⁻, dichromate Cr₂O₇²⁻, permanganate MnO₄⁻, peroxide O₂²⁻, thiocyanate SCN⁻; common transition metal ions (Cu⁺/Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, Ag⁺, Zn²⁺…); **-ite/-ate** and **hypo-/per-** series |

The oxyanion pattern (using chlorine): **hypochlorite ClO⁻, chlorite ClO₂⁻, chlorate ClO₃⁻, perchlorate ClO₄⁻**. Likewise: nitrite NO₂⁻ / nitrate NO₃⁻, sulfite SO₃²⁻ / sulfate SO₄²⁻, phosphite PO₃³⁻ / phosphate PO₄³⁻.

**Stoichiometry:** moles = mass / molar mass; use the balanced equation's mole ratios; find the **limiting reagent**; **percent yield = actual / theoretical × 100%**.

**Significant figures:** multiply/divide → keep the fewest sig figs; add/subtract → keep the fewest decimal places. Read glassware to **one estimated digit beyond the smallest marking**. For example, a 100 mL cylinder marked every 1 mL reads to 0.1 mL (like 43.6 mL). Always read the bottom of the **meniscus** at eye level.

## Gases

### Kinetic molecular theory

An ideal gas is made of tiny particles with negligible volume, in constant random motion, with elastic collisions and no attractions. Average kinetic energy is proportional to **Kelvin temperature**: KE_avg = (3/2)kT.

**Real gases** deviate at **high pressure** (molecules' volume matters) and **low temperature** (attractions matter). The **van der Waals equation** corrects for both:

(P + a n²/V²)(V − n b) = nRT

where **a** accounts for attractions (bigger for polar or large molecules) and **b** for molecule volume.

### The gas laws

![Boyle's law: pressure vs volume](img:chem-boyle)

| Law | Relationship | Held constant | Graph |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Boyle's** | P₁V₁ = P₂V₂ | n, T | P vs V is a hyperbola; P vs 1/V is a straight line |
| **Charles's** | V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ | n, P | V vs T (K) is a line through absolute zero |
| **Gay-Lussac's** | P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂ | n, V | P vs T (K) is a line |
| **Avogadro's** | V₁/n₁ = V₂/n₂ | P, T | Equal volumes hold equal moles |
| **Combined** | P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ | n | |
| **Ideal gas law** | **PV = nRT** | | |
| **Dalton's** | P_total = P₁ + P₂ + …; Pᵢ = xᵢ P_total | | xᵢ = mole fraction |
| **Graham's** | rate₁ / rate₂ = √(M₂ / M₁) | | Lighter gases effuse faster |

**Always use Kelvin**: K = °C + 273.15.

**Values of R** (choose the one that matches your units):

| R | Units |
|---|---|
| 0.08206 | L·atm / (mol·K) |
| 8.314 | J / (mol·K) = L·kPa / (mol·K) |
| 62.36 | L·mmHg / (mol·K) |

**Pressure units:** 1 atm = 101.325 kPa = 760 mmHg = 760 torr = 14.7 psi.

**STP** (Standard Temperature and Pressure): 0 °C and 1 atm, where 1 mol of an ideal gas is **22.4 L**. (IUPAC's newer STP uses 1 bar, giving 22.7 L. Use whatever the test says.)

### Useful forms of the ideal gas law

- **Molar mass:** M = mRT / (PV)
- **Density:** d = PM / (RT). Denser gases have higher molar mass, and density falls as temperature rises.

> [!TIP]
> Worked example: a 0.250 g gas sample fills 125 mL at 25.0 °C and 0.985 atm. n = PV/RT = (0.985)(0.125)/[(0.08206)(298.15)] = 5.03 × 10⁻³ mol. M = 0.250 / 0.00503 = 49.7 g/mol.

### Collecting a gas over water

![Collecting gas over water](img:chem-gas-over-water)

When you collect a gas by water displacement, the gas is mixed with water vapor. Use Dalton's law:

**P_gas = P_atm − P_water vapor(T)**

Water vapor pressure at common temperatures:

| T (°C) | 20 | 21 | 22 | 23 | 24 | 25 | 26 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P (mmHg) | 17.5 | 18.7 | 19.8 | 21.1 | 22.4 | 23.8 | 25.2 |

If the water level inside the collecting tube is higher than outside, the gas pressure is lower than atmospheric. Level them before reading, or correct for the water column height (mm of water ÷ 13.6 = mmHg).

### Classic gas labs

| Lab | Method | Key calculation |
|---|---|---|
| **Molar mass of butane** | Weigh a lighter, release gas into an inverted water-filled graduated cylinder, dry and reweigh | P_butane = P_atm − P_H₂O; n = PV/RT; M = Δm/n (theoretical 58.12 g/mol) |
| **Molar volume of H₂** | React a measured length of Mg ribbon with HCl in a eudiometer | Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂; moles H₂ = moles Mg; correct to STP |
| **Boyle's law** | Syringe with a pressure sensor | Plot P vs 1/V: straight line, slope = nRT |
| **Gay-Lussac's/absolute zero** | Flask in water baths of different temperatures with a pressure sensor | Extrapolate the P vs T line to P = 0 to estimate absolute zero (−273 °C) |
| **Charles's law** | Syringe or capillary tube in hot and cold baths | V vs T line |
| **Graham's law** | NH₃ and HCl from opposite ends of a glass tube form a white NH₄Cl ring | Ring is closer to HCl (heavier, slower): distance ratio ≈ √(36.46/17.03) ≈ 1.46 |
| **Gas density** | Mass of a flask before and after filling with a gas | d = m/V; compare with PM/RT |

**Sources of error:** a lighter that's still wet when weighed (mass loss looks bigger, so M is too large); not correcting for water vapor; temperature of the water vs the gas; reading the meniscus wrong; gas leaking or dissolving.

### Greenhouse gases and the ozone layer

![Greenhouse effect diagram](img:chem-greenhouse)

**Greenhouse effect:** sunlight (mostly visible) warms Earth's surface, which re-emits **infrared**. Greenhouse gases absorb that IR and re-radiate some of it back down. Molecules absorb IR only if their vibrations **change the dipole moment**, so CO₂, H₂O, CH₄, N₂O and CFCs are greenhouse gases, while N₂, O₂ and Ar aren't.

| Gas | Main sources | Global warming potential (100-year, relative to CO₂) | Lifetime |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CO₂** | Fossil fuels, deforestation, cement | 1 | Centuries+ |
| **CH₄** (methane) | Livestock, rice, wetlands, landfills, natural gas leaks | ~28–30 | ~12 years |
| **N₂O** (nitrous oxide) | Fertilizers, manure | ~265–273 | ~110 years |
| **CFCs and HFCs** | Refrigerants, aerosols (CFCs now banned) | Thousands | Decades to centuries |
| **H₂O** | Evaporation (a feedback, not a direct driver) | — | Days |

CO₂ has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialization to over 420 ppm. The **Keeling curve** (Mauna Loa, since 1958) shows that rise plus a yearly wiggle (Northern Hemisphere plant growth in summer pulls CO₂ down).

**Ozone:** in the **stratosphere**, ozone (O₃) absorbs UV-B and UV-C (the Chapman cycle: O₂ + UV → 2O; O + O₂ → O₃; O₃ + UV → O₂ + O). **CFCs** drift up, UV breaks off Cl atoms, and chlorine destroys ozone catalytically:

- Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂
- ClO + O → Cl + O₂
- Net: O₃ + O → 2O₂ (one Cl atom can destroy ~100,000 O₃)

The **Antarctic ozone hole** forms each spring because polar stratospheric clouds free up chlorine during the dark winter. The **Montreal Protocol (1987)** phased out CFCs, and the ozone layer is recovering (expected around 2066 over Antarctica). Ozone at ground level (the **troposphere**) is a pollutant that forms smog from NOx and VOCs in sunlight.

Ozone is measured in **Dobson units** (the hole is below 220 DU).

## Kinetics

### Reaction rates

**Rate** = change in concentration / change in time. For aA + bB → cC + dD:

rate = −(1/a) Δ[A]/Δt = −(1/b) Δ[B]/Δt = (1/c) Δ[C]/Δt = (1/d) Δ[D]/Δt

**What changes rates (collision theory):** a reaction needs collisions with **enough energy** (≥ activation energy) and **proper orientation**.

| Factor | Effect | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Concentration** (or pressure for gases) | ↑ rate | More collisions per second |
| **Temperature** | ↑ rate (often ~2× per 10 °C) | More molecules have energy above Eₐ (Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution shifts) and collide more often |
| **Surface area** (particle size) | Smaller particles → ↑ rate | More exposed surface for collisions |
| **Catalyst** | ↑ rate, not consumed | Provides a different pathway with **lower activation energy**; doesn't change ΔH or equilibrium |
| Nature of reactants | Ionic reactions in solution are fast; breaking strong covalent bonds is slow | |

![Energy diagram with and without a catalyst](img:chem-catalyst)

**Common rate labs** (all levels):

- **Iodine clock:** a solution suddenly turns blue-black when thiosulfate is used up. Time to color change ∝ 1/rate.
- **Alka-Seltzer or Mg + HCl:** measure CO₂ or H₂ volume over time, or time until a tablet dissolves at different temperatures, particle sizes or concentrations.
- **Sodium thiosulfate + HCl:** forms a cloudy sulfur precipitate; time until an "X" under the flask disappears.
- **Decomposition of H₂O₂:** catalyzed by yeast (catalase), MnO₂ or KI; measure O₂ volume or pressure (the "elephant toothpaste" reaction).
- **Crystal violet + NaOH:** a colorimeter or spectrophotometer tracks fading color (Beer's law: A = εbc, so absorbance ∝ concentration).

### Rate laws (State and National)

**Differential rate law:** rate = k[A]^m[B]^n. The orders m and n come from **experiment**, not the balanced equation (unless the step is elementary). The overall order is m + n.

**Finding orders with the method of initial rates:**

| Trial | [A] (M) | [B] (M) | Initial rate (M/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.10 | 0.10 | 2.0 × 10⁻³ |
| 2 | 0.20 | 0.10 | 8.0 × 10⁻³ |
| 3 | 0.10 | 0.20 | 4.0 × 10⁻³ |

Doubling [A] (trial 1 → 2) quadruples the rate, so it's **second order in A**. Doubling [B] (1 → 3) doubles the rate, so **first order in B**. Rate = k[A]²[B]. Using trial 1: k = 2.0 × 10⁻³ / (0.10² × 0.10) = **2.0 M⁻²s⁻¹**.

**Integrated rate laws:**

| Order | Integrated law | Linear plot | Slope | Half-life | Units of k |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | [A] = [A]₀ − kt | [A] vs t | −k | [A]₀ / 2k | M s⁻¹ |
| **1** | ln[A] = ln[A]₀ − kt | **ln[A] vs t** | −k | **0.693 / k** (constant) | s⁻¹ |
| **2** | 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt | **1/[A] vs t** | +k | 1 / (k[A]₀) | M⁻¹ s⁻¹ |

**Units of k** for overall order n: M^(1−n) s⁻¹.

To find the order from data, plot [A], ln[A] and 1/[A] against time. Whichever is a **straight line** tells you the order. A graphing calculator's linear regression (look for r² closest to 1) speeds this up.

> [!TIP]
> Example: a first-order reaction has k = 0.0462 min⁻¹. Its half-life is 0.693 / 0.0462 = 15.0 min. After 45 min (3 half-lives), 1/8 = 12.5% remains.

### Activation energy (State and National)

**Arrhenius equation:** k = A e^(−Eₐ/RT)

- **Linear form:** ln k = −(Eₐ/R)(1/T) + ln A. Plot ln k vs 1/T: slope = −Eₐ/R.
- **Two-point form:** **ln(k₂/k₁) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂)**, with R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) and T in kelvin.

> [!TIP]
> Example: k doubles from 25 °C (298 K) to 35 °C (308 K). ln 2 = (Eₐ/8.314)(1/298 − 1/308) → 0.693 = (Eₐ/8.314)(1.09 × 10⁻⁴) → Eₐ ≈ 52.9 kJ/mol.

### Mechanisms (State and National)

A **mechanism** is a series of **elementary steps** that add up to the overall reaction. For an elementary step, the rate law follows directly from its molecularity (A + B → … gives rate = k[A][B]).

- **Intermediates** are made in one step and used in a later one (they don't appear in the overall equation). **Catalysts** are used and then regenerated.
- The **rate-determining step** is the slowest step, and it sets the overall rate law.
- A mechanism is **valid** if (1) its steps add up to the overall equation and (2) its predicted rate law matches the experimental one. If the slow step contains an intermediate, replace it using a fast equilibrium step before it.

> [!TIP]
> Example: 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂, experimental rate = k[NO]²[O₂]. Mechanism: (1) 2NO ⇌ N₂O₂ (fast), (2) N₂O₂ + O₂ → 2NO₂ (slow). Slow step rate = k₂[N₂O₂][O₂]. From the fast equilibrium, [N₂O₂] = K[NO]². So rate = k₂K[NO]²[O₂], matching experiment.

**Reaction energy diagrams:** each step has its own hump. The highest hump (from the start) is the rate-determining step. Intermediates sit in the valleys between humps. **Transition states** are at the peaks.

## Probeware and data analysis

Supervisors may demonstrate sensors such as gas pressure, temperature, colorimeter or spectrophotometer, CO₂, O₂ and pH probes, often with Vernier or PASCO software. Then they'll give you data. Know how to:

- Plot with the right axes, and linearize (P vs 1/V, ln[A] vs t, ln k vs 1/T).
- Find slopes and intercepts, and give them units.
- Calculate **percent error** = |experimental − accepted| / accepted × 100%.
- Name realistic sources of error (and which way they push your answer).

## Lab skills checklist

- Reading meniscus (bottom for water, at eye level); burets read top-down to 0.01 mL.
- Taring a balance; weighing by difference.
- Using a eudiometer or inverted graduated cylinder for gas collection.
- Diluting solutions: **M₁V₁ = M₂V₂**.
- Consistent timing (the same person starts and stops the timer).
- Waste disposal as instructed, and **clean up**: up to 10% is at stake.

**Equipment list:** check the current Division C Chemistry Lab Equipment List on soinc.org (and the Calculator Class descriptions). It typically includes items like a beaker or flask set, graduated cylinders, test tubes and rack, thermometer, stirring rod, droppers, spatula, wash bottle, timer and goggles. Bring a labeled kit box.

## Notes sheet tips

Gas law table and R values, water vapor pressure table, the integrated rate law table with half-lives and units of k, Arrhenius equations, ion list (oxyanions), GWP table and ozone reactions, a worked example of each calculation type.

## Common pitfalls

- Using °C instead of K.
- Forgetting to subtract water vapor pressure.
- Getting rate-law orders from coefficients of the overall equation.
- Wrong units for k.
- Forgetting that catalysts don't change equilibrium or ΔH.
- Ignoring the cleanup instructions.

## Test yourself

<details><summary>1. A gas at 2.00 atm and 300 K is heated to 450 K at constant volume. New pressure?</summary>

P₂ = 2.00 × (450/300) = 3.00 atm.

</details>

<details><summary>2. Which effuses faster, He or CH₄, and by how much?</summary>

He: rate ratio = √(16.04/4.00) ≈ 2.00, so helium is about twice as fast.

</details>

<details><summary>3. ln[A] vs time is a straight line with slope −0.0231 s⁻¹. Order, k and half-life?</summary>

First order, k = 0.0231 s⁻¹, t½ = 0.693/0.0231 = 30.0 s.

</details>

<details><summary>4. Hydrogen is collected over water at 25 °C with P_atm = 755 mmHg. What's the pressure of the H₂?</summary>

755 − 23.8 = 731 mmHg.

</details>

<details><summary>5. Why is N₂ not a greenhouse gas?</summary>

Its symmetric stretch doesn't change its dipole moment, so it doesn't absorb infrared.

</details>

<details><summary>6. What are the units of k for a third-order overall reaction?</summary>

M⁻² s⁻¹.

</details>
